# *Game Wrap* Vol. 1 (2016): reading notes

Back to [[eduLARP Resource Drive]] · Source: [[GameWrapVol1.pdf]] (113 pp.) · Publisher: New England Interactive Literature (NEIL); editor-in-chief Viktoriya Fuzaylova · [web](https://gamewrap.interactiveliterature.org/vol1/designing-a-larp-for-educational-purposes)

**Short answer:** two pieces matter most for MIHLab. The first is Muriel Algayres's **design article** and **full game text** for *Graveyard of the Sacrifice*, a WWII eduLARP for 16 eleventh-graders in France. It's the most classroom-realistic eduLARP in the drive, because it ran inside ordinary school constraints with parents signing off. The second is Kat Davis's **"Beyond Birdseed,"** a catalog of *non-combat* encounter types that fits Scouting's no-striking rule.

## 1. *Graveyard of the Sacrifice* (Algayres, pp. 7–11 and 87ff.)

| | |
|---|---|
| Players | 16 students, 11th-grade History, taught in English (a French "European section" class) |
| Theme | "Memory, duty, survivors' guilt and hope for peace" |
| Setting | **1951**, after the war. The author chose this so students wouldn't gloss over, or have to play, war crimes and collaboration: "too harsh a role for underage participants" |
| Running time | About 6 hours: **3 × 45-minute pre-game workshops**, about **2 hours** of play, a **45-minute** exit workshop and debrief |
| Hook | A real **memorial visit** (Mont-Valérien), done *out* of character for legal reasons, which then fed into the characters' views |
| Play | A meeting or picnic, partly **outdoors and off school grounds** |
| Teacher's role | An NPC, "Ms Greene," who interviews each student in character so quieter students get a turn |
| Characters | Short sheets with **pre-set relationships**, using a Danish template (Rollespilsfabrikken / *Fairweather Manor*) used with permission |
| Assessment | A **written piece in the character's voice** afterward (letters, newspaper articles, journals), plus a questionnaire a week later |

### The planning categories
The article analyzes the game using Myriel Balzer's framework from "Learning by playing" (*Knudepunkt 2015 Companion Book*; the article spells her name two ways):
1. **Constraints:** class periods of about 45 working minutes, and activities should stay under 20 minutes, so the game is broken into short scenes.
2. **Project planning:** introduced **two months ahead**, with a two-page design document for families, parents' signed permission and admin sign-off. "No concern or opposition" followed.
3. **Learning content:** set the knowledge and skills first. In eduLARP, learning content, not theme, drives the design.
4. **Storytelling:** choose a frame that keeps young players out of morally unplayable roles.
5. **External setup:** a real-world site visit anchors the fiction.
6. **Game design:** a one-page summary for players, with details explained in class.
7. **Documents:** short character sheets with existing relationships.

### Problems it planned for
- **Rejection** (a player refuses their character): prevent it with **short role-play exercises beforehand**, a full explanation, workshops, and **loose character templates players can change**.
- **Breaking character:** rare; a quick in-game reminder was enough.
- **Fatigue:** the game was stopped early, before it fell apart.
- **Playing in public:** players felt some tension being watched, but no one had to interact with outsiders in character.

### Why it worked, according to the author
- **The character's alibi:** playing a character lowers the fear of getting it wrong, so students are "more daring."
- **Motivation vs. incentive:** motivation is the novelty, the grade and the fun. Incentives are the in-game hooks: ideology, family ties and the knowledge that they'd write afterward in their character's voice.

⚠ **Secondhand citation:** the article quotes Bowman & Standiford (2015), *Educational Larp in the Middle School Classroom* (*IJRP*), as finding that students' intrinsic motivation and interest in science rose significantly. **Read the original before citing it** in funder materials.

## 2. "Beyond Birdseed: Bringing Immersion to Non-Combat Modules" (Kat Davis, p. 25)
The thesis is that what brings live-action games to life is "**What You See Is What You Get**": if you want to do something, you physically do it. Combat is only one way to do that.

| Encounter type | What players do | Trail version for The Quest |
|---|---|---|
| **Rogue module** | Sneak past guards; open locks; disarm traps; real tasks done quietly | Cross a "sleeping dragon" campsite silently; untie a knotted "lock"; a navigation puzzle under time pressure |
| **Talky: diplomacy** | Mediate between factions; serve as a jury; reconcile a split group | Two NPC villages at odds over a spring; the party has to broker the peace |
| **Talky: masquerade and social** | Win information or favor through talk | Persuade the gatekeeper NPC; trade for supplies |

Key design point: these work best when players **already care** about the NPCs or the outcome, and when the possible outcomes are clear.

## 4. Takeaways for The Quest
1. **The parent packet:** copy Algayres's sequence. Introduce the trip two months ahead with a two-page design document and signed permission. This doubles as the church-partner pitch.
2. **Warm up first:** run short role-play exercises at den meetings *before* the trip, to head off rejection.
3. **Loose characters:** pre-set relationships (parent and child linked), with freedom to change the rest.
4. **Choose a safe frame:** like setting *Graveyard* in 1951, pick story frames that never ask kids to play evil acts. Moral weight should come from the *choices*, which suits the token board.
5. **Writing in character as integration:** the journey journal written as the character, plus a letter home in the character's voice.
6. **The GM as interviewer:** the DM or Guide makes sure every quiet child gets a scene.
7. **Non-combat encounter types** as the backbone of trail encounters, with dice saved for true danger.

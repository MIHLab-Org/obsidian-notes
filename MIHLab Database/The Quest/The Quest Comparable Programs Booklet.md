# The Quest Wilderness Ministry: Program Research and Planning Guide

A survey of existing programs that combine outdoor adventure, role-playing games (tabletop D&D or live-action role-play), and character formation. Prepared as background research for The Quest, a proposed program that takes young people on a backpacking or bikepacking trip where a hired game master turns the whole journey into one live-action D&D campaign.

**Summary finding:** No program found does exactly what The Quest proposes: a multi-day, moving expedition where the real miles, weather and fatigue become part of a single continuous campaign. Existing programs are almost all day camps or base camps, where the journey is imaginary and the setting is a park or campsite. The examples below each cover part of the idea.

## 1. Dust & Tribe — D&T Guild: Dungeons and Dragons Campout

**Why it matters:** Closest in spirit. A faith-community outdoor organization that pairs D&D with camping.

**What it is:**

- A weekend campout (example dates: August 1–3) priced at $430.
- Open to men and women 13+; "All creeds welcome."
- Rated "Adventure Score: 2" on the organization's difficulty scale.
- Held at D&T Camp One, with two nights of accommodation and four hosted halal meals (dinners and breakfasts).
- Format: a hosted dinner over an open fire, then an outdoor role-playing adventure; the next morning, a farewell breakfast and a local hike with the Camp One goats.

**Gap relative to The Quest:** Base-camp format, short duration, the game is an evening activity rather than the structure of the whole trip.

**Links:**

- D&T Guild D&D Campout event page: https://dustandtribe.com/?p=6333
- Related campout itinerary page: https://dustandtribe.com/?p=7627

## 2. AYF Camp Miniwanca — Worlds of Adventure: LARP and Dungeons & Dragons

**Why it matters:** Closest match on the character-building side. AYF (American Youth Foundation) is a long-standing character and leadership development organization.

**What it is:**

- Run within Miniwanca's Boys Camp (Michigan), reported July 2025.
- Combined live-action role-play and tabletop D&D.
- When a character died, players had to adapt, create a new character, and re-enter the world.
- A standout moment: the Boys Camp Director stepped in as a surprise villain, showing staff acting directly inside the fiction.

**Gap relative to The Quest:** Takes place at a resident camp, not on an expedition.

**Link:**

- AYF article: https://ayf.com/?p=37893

## 3. Strathcona County (Alberta) — Nature's Quest: D&D and outdoor camp weeks

**Why it matters:** A public recreation program that pairs D&D with wilderness skills, and in some weeks a backcountry overnight.

**What it is:**

- "Nature's Quest: D&D" for ages 10–13: "Let the quest begin! We are waiting for you to join the Dungeon Masters who are ready to take you on a unique adventure."
- Camp listings describe playing a cooperative, strategy-based fantasy role-playing game where campers take on characters to complete a quest, alongside regular camp activities.
- No prior D&D experience required; basics are taught.
- Related weeks in the same program include fire cooking, wilderness skills, indoor rock climbing, and an overnight hike into the backcountry.
- Based at the county's wilderness centre.

**Gap relative to The Quest:** D&D and the backcountry overnight are parallel activities, not a single integrated campaign.

## 5. Renaissance Adventures — Adventure Quest (Boulder, Colorado)

**Why it matters:** The best model for the live-action game master role, and for deliberately building moral dilemmas into play.

**What it is:**

- Summer day camps for ages 6–17 in which kids play heroes with foam swords on weeklong quests in local parks and trails.
- "Quest leaders" supervise each group and act as the link between the kids and the world, describing what they see and acting out what they encounter, while kids keep total freedom in their in-game choices, with real consequences.
- Director Mark Hoge: "We put all sorts of challenges into the quest—social challenges, moral dilemmas... Underneath it all we get to help them learn."
- Program variants: Star Quest (sci-fi), Quest and Quill (questing plus creative writing), and Heroes Academy (more in-depth, open-ended questing for teens).
- Teens can attend quest-leading workshops and become Counselors-in-Training.
- Safety is addressed first in each session.

**Gap relative to The Quest:** Day-camp format; quests happen in local parks rather than on a multi-day route.

## 7. Adjacent fields worth borrowing from

- **Wilderness therapy:** JMU's Counseling Center and UREC run trips where students backpack and take part in outdoor education sessions and therapy process groups along the way. https://jmu.edu/counselingctr/services/wilderness-therapy.shtml
- **Therapeutic role-play:** Vassar's "Dungeons and Therapy" group has participants embody their ideal self through a fictional persona. https://offices.vassar.edu/counseling-service/services/group-counseling/dungeons-and-therapy/
- **Wilderness travel rules for game masters:** published supplements such as LoreSmyth's *Wondrous Expeditions: Forests* and the D&D *Dungeon Master's Screen: Wilderness Kit* provide expedition and travel mechanics that could be mapped onto real trail days.

## 8. Where The Quest is distinctive

- **Real journey as game structure:** actual distance, terrain, weather and fatigue serve as the campaign's travel mechanics.
- **Single continuous arc:** one story across a multi-day expedition, rather than one-shots or daily sessions.
- **Character formation framing:** explicit roots in wilderness ministry and moral imagination.

**Practical challenges to plan for:** insurance and liability, wilderness-certified staff (first aid, trip leading), and finding a game master who can also lead outdoors, or pairing a game master with a trip leader.

## 10. How insurance is handled

**Short answer:** None of the five programs publish their insurance arrangements. A dedicated niche of LARP insurers does exist, though, and the standard package they sell is an **accident medical policy plus a general liability policy**, bundled annually.

### 10.1 Why insurance matters for this kind of program

Rookie Insurance sums up the risk: participants can get hurt, families may have limited medical coverage or high deductibles, and game masters can face personal exposure to lawsuits. Venues and landowners also often require proof of liability coverage. Westpoint Insurance notes that most LARP injuries happen during staged battles, which matters if The Quest uses foam-sword combat. An expedition adds trail, water, weather and vehicle risks on top of that.

### 10.3 What this means for a Scouting pilot

If the pilot runs as an official den or pack activity, Scouting America's own unit coverage and safety rules may apply instead of a separate policy. This was not verified in this search. Confirm with your local council before planning, especially whether a hired outside game master counts as covered, and whether foam combat and bikepacking are approved activities for your son's age group. A later MIHLab-run version of The Quest outside Scouting would need its own accident and liability coverage from a provider like those above.

### 10.4 Questions to ask each operator about insurance

1. Do you carry a LARP-specific policy or a general camp policy, and through which broker?
2. Does your accident medical coverage pay first or after the family's own insurance?
3. How are foam-weapon combat and off-site trail activities covered?
4. Are contracted game masters or actors covered, or must they carry their own coverage?
5. What do landowners, parks or host camps require from you?
6. What waivers do you use, and has your lawyer reviewed them for minors?
7. Did ACA accreditation or other standards change your premiums or requirements?

# Part III: Filling the Gaps

This part answers the open questions left in Part II: the Scouting rules, Strathcona County contacts, the smaller programs, staffing and pay, and safety and waivers. **The most important finding is that Scouting America's Guide to Safe Scouting prohibits LARP-style combat.** A Scouting pilot of The Quest must use non-contact role-play.

## 13. Scouting America: rules that shape a pilot

### 13.1 LARP combat is a prohibited activity

The Guide to Safe Scouting's activity planning section lists prohibited activities. They include "activities where participants strike at each other, including martial arts, boxing, combat games, gladiator games, and reenactment activities such as live action role-playing games (LARP) and Society for Creative Anachronism (SCA) activities." It also prohibits activities where participants shoot or throw objects at each other, such as dodgeball or sock fights.

**What this means for The Quest:** foam-sword fighting between Scouts is out. Story, characters, puzzles, negotiation, riddles, map work and dice-resolved "combat" told at the campfire all remain open. That actually suits a tabletop D&D style better than a boffer LARP style.

Source: [Guide to Safe Scouting, Activity Planning and Risk Assessment](https://www.scouting.org/health-and-safety/gss/gss07/)

### 13.2 Insurance coverage through Scouting

| Coverage | What it does | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| General liability, registered adults | Primary liability coverage for registered volunteers and professionals for claims arising from an official Scouting activity | [GSS Insurance](https://www.scouting.org/health-and-safety/gss/gss10/) |
| General liability, unregistered volunteers | Excess (secondary) coverage only | [Montana Council explainer](https://scoutingmontana.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/11/BSA-Insurance.pdf) |
| Charter organization coverage | Commercial general liability including excess auto liability; written at $7.5 million per occurrence and $20 million aggregate in the current charter agreement | [Annual Charter Agreement (PDF)](https://www.scouting.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/524-95626-Annual-Charter-Agreement.pdf) |
| Accident and sickness | Many councils cover youth and registered adults for injuries on official outings (confirm your council's plan) | [Crossroads of America example](https://www.joinscoutsin.org/resources/forms/insurance-forms/) |
| Certificates of insurance | Councils can issue certificates naming parks or landowners as additional insured; facility contracts need careful review | [Certificates of Insurance](https://www.scouting.org/health-and-safety/safety-moments/certificates-of-insurance/) |

**A hired game master is a vendor.** Scouting America's Youth Protection FAQ says third-party vendors don't have to register, but "must be licensed businesses that carry proper liability insurance." Supervision stays with the unit leaders. So a hired DM should have their own business and liability policy, or should register as a Scouting volunteer instead. Source: [Youth Protection FAQs](https://www.scouting.org/health-and-safety/yp-faqs/)

### 13.3 Leader requirements and transferring your registration

- **Safeguarding Youth training** is required for all registered volunteers and must be renewed every year. Source: [Youth Protection](https://www.scouting.org/training/safeguarding-youth/)
- **The 72-hour rule:** any adult who spends 72 or more total hours with a unit on Scouting activities must register, with a background check and youth protection training, even if they're a parent. A multi-day Quest could trigger this for a hired DM. Source: [Registration Guidebook (PDF)](https://quivira.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/25/2021/01/Registration-Guidebook.pdf)
- **Transferring your registration:** adults transfer themselves in my.scouting.org through the "My Applications" tab. You don't need a new application, but you must answer the youth protection questions again. Sources: [Transfers and Multiples job aid (PDF)](https://www.scouting.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/Transfer-and-Multiples-for-My.Scouting-Job-Ai.pdf) · [Service Center FAQ](https://confluence.scouting.org/display/BSAKB/Transfer+and+Multiples+in+My+Scouting+FAQ)
- **Supervision:** two-deep adult leadership is required.

### 13.4 Bikepacking and backpacking in Scouting

- Full high-adventure trekking (three or more consecutive nights in the wilderness, including backpacking and mountain biking) is aimed at older Scouts, not Cub Scouts. Source: [Guide to Safe Scouting (PDF)](https://scoutingevent.com/Download/80372330/OR/Guide_to_Safe_Scouting.pdf)
- The Cub Scout Cycling adventure already includes planning and riding a 10-mile route with a patrol, pack or family. That makes it a natural first leg for a mini-Quest. Source: [Cycling adventure](https://www.scouting.org/cub-scout-adventures/cycling/)
- Check the age-appropriate activity guidelines before choosing a format. Source: [Age-Appropriate Guidelines (PDF)](https://www.joinscoutsin.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/Age-Appropriate-Guidelines-Scouting.pdf)

## 17. Safety, waivers and foam weapons

### 17.1 Foam weapon standards (outside Scouting only)

Across LARP groups the common standards are: a PVC, fiberglass or carbon-fiber core; at least 5/8 inch of closed-cell foam around it; foam extending at least 1 inch past each end of the core; and a soft open-cell thrusting tip of 2–3 inches, so the core can't be felt. Groups inspect weapons at check-in, ban strikes with the flat or the pommel, and require checking for damage before each use.

Sources: [Xadune](https://xadune.com/getting-started/larp-weapons/) · [Mythic Adventures](https://www.mythicadventures.org/weapons/safety) · [Citadel LARP](https://citadel-larp.com/props-and-gear-guidelines) · [Underworld LARP guide (PDF)](https://underworldlarp.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/Underworld_LARP_Weapon_and_Armour_Guide_v1.4.pdf)

# Part IV: Creative Design

This part has two halves. Section 19 describes how existing programs design their stories and game systems, with sources. Sections 20 to 26 are original design proposals for The Quest itself. They are starting points to test and revise, not findings.

## 19. How existing programs design stories and game systems

### 19.1 Renaissance Adventures: Adventure Quest

- **Archetypes and powers:** participants pick mythic archetypes and powers, then band together to defend a village, solve a mystery or negotiate a peace treaty.
- **Small parties:** groups of about six, so every child gets a turn to "shine and be the hero."
- **Planned social learning:** social and emotional learning is built in, and "many of the plot points are planned to develop the participants' social skills."
- **Challenge types:** riddles, map reading, puzzling dilemmas, negotiation and swordplay.
- **Supporting materials:** the trademark list in its parents' guide names a Journeybook, a quest-leading workshop and three levels of "Inspiring Invitations" (details not public). The guide also mentions an "Asking for Help" talk.
- **Tailoring:** quests are adjusted to the goals and needs of each group.

Sources: [Adventure Quest program](https://www.renaissanceadventures.com/kids-camps-boulder-denver/program-types/adventure-quest/) · [Tour of Adventure Quest](https://www.renaissanceadventures.com/about/tour-adventure-quest/) · [Homeschool programs blog](https://www.renaissanceadventures.com/blog/2024/08/29/homeschool-enrichment-programs/) · [2017 Parents' Guide (PDF)](http://www.renaissanceadventures.com/pdfs/2017ParentsGuide.pdf)

### 19.2 The Story School: Camp FABLE and Camp Grimoire

- **Story sources:** each world draws on real history, mythology and literature, so campers "live out a story they will read about in school."
- **Learning drives power:** campers earn strength points and magical abilities by completing STEAM lessons, then use them against monsters.
- **Three strands:** story carries content; leveling up requires STEM skills; role-play teaches social and emotional skills.
- **Mix of challenges:** foam battles, puzzles woven into the story, and escape-room-style challenges.
- **Older campers:** Grimoire raises the difficulty for teens with fiercer monsters, harder puzzles and more "cerebral" challenges.

Sources: [Homepage](https://www.thestoryschool.org/) · [Camp page](https://www.thestoryschool.org/camp) · [Worcester Central listing](https://worcestercentralkidscalendar.com/the-story-school/)

### 19.3 Østerskov Efterskole (Denmark): a whole school taught through larp

- Teaching is organized as multi-week projects, each with a narrative storyline binding the activities together, rather than subjects.
- The school year cycles through narrative weeks, test and project weeks, and exam preparation.
- Academic content becomes a practical problem in the story: a spaceship's navigation computer fails, and the only way to plot a course is to solve a differential equation.
- Students may add their own plot points, in the Nordic larp style.
- Research (Gjedde, 2013) found that some students are motivated by the emotional story and others by strategy and competition. Design for both.
- Contact: kontor@osterskov.dk · +45 7734 2990 · Kirketoften 4, 9500 Hobro, Denmark.

Sources: [Nordic Larp Wiki](https://www.nordiclarp.org/wiki/%C3%98sterskov_Efterskole) · [Learning by Playing](https://www.nordiclarp.org/2015/03/04/learning-by-playing-larp-as-a-teaching-method/) · [RPG Research profile](https://www2.rpgresearch.com/blog/osterskov-efterskole-danish-public-high-school-teaching-all-subjects-using-larp) · [Middle-school edu-larp study](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/333731559_Educational_Larp_in_the_Middle_School_Classroom_A_Mixed_Method_Case_Study)

**Two Nordic design ideas worth borrowing.** First, power between adults and children has to be designed on purpose. If it isn't, out-of-game social power carries straight into the game. Second, designing "for hope, agency, and inclusion" with young players. That second idea is closely aligned with MIHLab's moral-imagination-and-hope focus. Sources: [Power dynamics between adults and children](https://www.nordiclarp.org/2024/03/04/designing-power-dynamics-between-adults-and-children-in-larps/) · [Nordic Larp tools](https://www.nordiclarp.org/category/tools/)

### 19.5 Common patterns across all of these

1. A small party (about six) with one guide.
2. Real-world skill turned into in-game power.
3. Social and moral dilemmas planned into the plot, not left to chance.
4. Physical tokens of progress, such as a journal, a passport or abilities.
5. A departure-and-return structure that ends in recognition.

## 20. The Quest: core design concept

**Proposal:** the real route is the story map. Each physical stretch of trail is a region of the world, each campsite is a settlement or refuge, and the trip's actual hardships (climbs, rain, fatigue, a broken chain) are the travel events. Nobody pretends to journey. They really journey, and the story explains why.

**Design principles**

1. **The body earns, the story pays:** real effort and real Scout or trail skills unlock in-game abilities, adapting The Story School's STEAM-points model.
2. **Moral choices have weight:** every chapter contains at least one dilemma with no clean answer, building on Renaissance Adventures' planned dilemmas.
3. **Hope is the destination:** the arc ends in repair or restoration, not just victory, in keeping with MIHLab's emphasis on hope.
4. **Conflict through words, wit and dice:** no physical striking. This suits Scouting rules (Section 13.1) and keeps attention on moral imagination.
5. **Everyone shines:** each character has a moment designed around their strengths.

## 21. World and story frame

Three sample premises, each built to fit a route with a start, a hard middle and a destination:

- **The Lamplighters.** The lanterns along an old road have gone dark, and the villages between them have stopped trusting one another. The party carries the last flame to relight each lantern (each campsite). At every village they must restore some broken trust before the lantern will catch. Theme: hope as something carried and shared.
- **The Cartographers' Guild.** The great map of the realm was torn, and the pieces are scattered. The party travels to recover them, and each fragment comes with a moral riddle about what the map should show and who it leaves out. Theme: seeing truly, and the ethics of representation. This fits well with philosophy content.
- **The Pilgrims of the Spring.** A drought has struck the land, and the party journeys to a distant spring to ask why. At the end they learn the drought is tied to choices the villages made, and the "cure" is a change of heart they must carry home. Theme: responsibility and return. It suits the wilderness-ministry framing.

## 22. Campaign structure mapped to the trip

| Trip stage | Hero's journey stage | Game content | Formation focus |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Gathering (before departure) | Call to adventure | Character creation; the commission; packing as "provisioning" | Choosing who you want to be |
| Day 1, first miles | Crossing the threshold | Leaving the known lands; a gatekeeper character tests the party's resolve | Courage, commitment |
| Middle days | Trials and allies | Regions with puzzles, travelers in need, rival parties, negotiations | Cooperation, honesty, mercy |
| The hardest day (biggest climb or longest ride) | The ordeal | The story's crisis lands on the real hardest stretch | Perseverance, caring for the weakest member |
| Destination | The reward | The goal is reached, and with it a revelation that changes the meaning of the quest | Humility, insight |
| Return | Return with the gift | Bringing the gift home; each character is recognized | Service, gratitude |

**Nightly rhythm:** a short campfire session each evening resolves the day's events with dice, introduces the next region and closes with reflection. The trail days run in light live-action mode, with scenes at planned stops.

## 23. Game mechanics

**Character sheet:** a simplified one-page sheet with a name, an archetype, three virtues the player chooses to grow, two special abilities and a short backstory.

**Sample archetypes:** Pathfinder (navigation), Healer (first aid), Keeper of Lore (knowledge and riddles), Quartermaster (food and gear), Herald (negotiation and speech), Warden (protecting the group). Each one maps to a real responsibility on the trip, so the role is both a fiction and a job.

**Real skills earn in-game power:**

| Real-world action | In-game reward |
| --- | --- |
| Navigating a leg correctly by map | The Pathfinder gains a "True Path" ability |
| Fixing a flat or adjusting a bike | Earns a "Tinker's Mark" usable to repair a story artifact |
| Pitching camp as a team in a set time | The party's refuge is "warded" for the night |
| Cooking the group meal | Restores the party's "Heart" points |
| Helping a struggling member | Earns a virtue token (see below) |

**Virtue tokens:** adults award physical tokens (for example, carved wooden discs or bead colors) for observed acts of courage, honesty, kindness or perseverance. Tokens can be spent in the story for rerolls or special help. They also become keepsakes of the trip, playing a role like The Heroic Journey's stamped passport.

**Conflict resolution:** dice decide outcomes in the campfire sessions. On the trail, "encounters" are riddles, negotiations with characters played by staff, team challenges and moral choices. Nothing involves striking or throwing at people.

**Journey journal:** each youth keeps a small in-character journal of what happened, what they chose and why. It is the reflection tool, and a natural place for the faith or character-formation layer (a nightly question, a verse or a maxim).

## 24. Moral dilemma bank (samples)

1. **The Shortcut:** a stranger offers a route that saves half a day but crosses land the party was asked not to enter. (Honesty, keeping promises)
2. **The Slow Traveler:** an injured character (played by staff) needs escort, and taking them means missing a deadline in the story. (Mercy versus duty)
3. **The Shared Water:** the party finds a spring, and a rival party arrives just as thirsty. (Justice, generosity)
4. **The False Credit:** the village elder praises the wrong party member for a deed. (Humility, truthfulness)
5. **The Ancient Law:** a village rule seems cruel, but breaking it would cause harm the party can't see. (Respect, moral uncertainty)
6. **The Tired Friend:** in the real world, one member is struggling. The story offers an in-game reward to the group that waits for and supports them. (Care for the weakest)

Each dilemma ends with a short debrief question at the campfire: "What did your character value in that moment? What would you do?"

## 25. Roles and staffing for the story

- **Game master:** runs the story, plays key characters, adapts on the fly.
- **Trip leader:** owns safety, route, weather and timing, with authority to pause the game.
- **Character actors (optional):** volunteers or parents placed at checkpoints to play travelers, villagers or gatekeepers. Within Scouting, adults present 72 or more hours must register (Section 13.3).
- **The "pause" signal:** a clear, agreed word or gesture that stops the game for safety or comfort. Anyone, youth or adult, may use it.

**Safety tools from tabletop gaming:** session zero (agreeing on tone and limits before play), an "X-card" style signal to skip content that makes someone uncomfortable, and lines and veils (topics left out, or kept offstage).

## 26. Pilot design: a one-day den Quest

A sample first test for your son's den, within Scouting rules.

**Premise:** *The Lamplighters*, one lantern only.

| Time | Activity |
| --- | --- |
| Morning, at the meeting place | Quick character creation (archetype, name, one virtue); the commission from the "Lantern Warden" |
| Trail stop 1 | A riddle at a bridge or trail junction, solved by the Keeper of Lore with the group |
| Trail stop 2 | A "wounded traveler" (a parent in costume) needing first aid, which ties to a real Cub Scout skill |
| Trail stop 3 | A dilemma: a shortcut with a hidden cost |
| Lunch | The "Quartermaster" serves; the party's Heart is restored |
| Final stop | Relighting the lantern (a safe battery lantern or supervised fire if permitted) after the group recalls one act of kindness from the day |
| Closing | Virtue tokens awarded; each Scout says what their character learned |

**What to observe:** engagement levels, which stops worked, whether the story helped or hurt pacing, how parents responded, and whether the reflection felt natural or forced. Those observations become the case for a longer overnight or bikepacking version.

# Part V: Certifications for Running The Quest Yourself

To act as both game master and wilderness guide, the certifications that add the most legitimacy are, in order: **wilderness medicine (WFA, then WFR)**, **Leave No Trace**, **Scouting outdoor training**, a **cycling instructor credential** for bikepacking, and optionally a **game master facilitation** credential. Core wilderness credibility takes about two to four weeks of total course time, spread over a year.

# Part VI: Accreditation and Compliance for a Business

For an organization offering The Quest, two voluntary accreditations add the most legitimacy: **American Camp Association (ACA) accreditation**, which explicitly covers travel and trip programs, and **Association for Experiential Education (AEE) accreditation for adventure programs**. In Florida, the legal requirements are mainly Level 2 fingerprint background screening, a Department of Health permit if you operate a fixed camp site, and land-use permits for public lands. Florida does not license guides.

## 34. Voluntary accreditations

### 34.1 American Camp Association (ACA)

- **What it is:** ACA says it is the only national accrediting body for camps of all types. Its standards focus on health, safety and risk management, and government agencies use them as benchmarks.
- **Fit for The Quest:** ACA accredits day and resident camps, **travel and trip programs**, school and environmental education programs, and camps with special emphases, including religious ones.
- **Process:** a written self-assessment against the standards, then an on-site visit during operation. Some standards are mandatory. Applications must arrive by **February 1** for a visit that summer. The accreditation year runs November 1 to October 31.
- **First season:** a camp can be visited in its first season, but it isn't accredited until all criteria are confirmed.
- **Cost and time:** annual fees on a sliding scale by location. One industry estimate puts initial fees at $500 to $1,200 or more, with 40 to 80 hours of self-assessment work in the first year.
- **Standards book:** the *ACA Accreditation Process Guide* (2025 standards).

Links: [Program overview](https://www.acacamps.org/program-overview-accreditation) · [Accreditation](https://www.acacamps.org/accreditation) · [Getting started](https://www.acacamps.org/accreditation/overview/getting-started-accreditation) · [Camps seeking accreditation](https://www.acacamps.org/membership/camps/camps-seeking-accreditation) · [Standards at a glance](https://www.acacamps.org/accreditation/overview/standards-glance) · [Cost](https://www.acacamps.org/how-much-does-accreditation-cost) · [Site-visit timeline](https://www.acacamps.org/accreditation/accreditation-academy/timeline-site-visits) · [Process Guide](https://www.acacamps.org/book/aca-accreditation-process-guide) · [Jumbula cost estimate](https://blog.jumbula.com/camp-accreditation/)

### 34.2 Association for Experiential Education (AEE)

- **What it is:** since 1994, AEE's accreditation has been the industry's accepted standard for experiential and adventure programs, built on risk-management, ethics and quality standards and assessed by independent reviewers.
- **Two tracks:** *Adventure Programs* (the right fit for The Quest) and *Outdoor Behavioral Healthcare* (for programs with licensed therapists; not The Quest).
- **Process:** you study the standards manual, apply, host a site visit by peer reviewers, and the AEE Accreditation Council votes on accreditation.
- **Local note:** AEE is headquartered at 2315 18th Street South, St. Petersburg, FL 33712 · 303-440-8844, so it's in your own state.

Links: [About accreditation](https://www.aee.org/accreditation) · [Adventure program standards](https://www.aee.org/manual-of-accreditation-for-adventure-programs) · [Application and documents](https://www.aee.org/application-and-documents) · [Why choose an accredited program](https://www.aee.org/why-choose-an-accredited-program) · [One organization's path to accreditation](https://ncoae.org/blog/working-way-toward-aee-accreditation/)

### 34.3 Which to pursue

| --- | --- | --- |

## 42. What the family programs teach for The Quest's design

1. **Rituals of blessing and recognition** (Voice of Wilderness, Blueprint for Men) fit naturally at the end of the campaign, when each character is recognized by their own parent.
2. **Nightly structured discussion** (Faithful Fathering) maps onto the campfire debrief after each day's dilemma.
3. **Cohorts of about four families** (Blueprint) suit both trail logistics and a D&D party size.
4. **Before-and-after sessions** (Blueprint's 7 weeks, YMCA's school year) would let character creation start at home and the story's "return" continue after the trip.
5. **Parents as participants on their own journey** (Back To Earth) suggests giving parents real characters and roles, not just supervision.
6. **Two difficulty tracks** (Camp Mondamin) handle mixed ages and fitness.
7. **Pre-trip family interviews** (Inner Guide) help the game master write each family's story threads.
8. **A continuing world** (Camp de Benneville's "Year Two") gives families a reason to return each year.

### 42.1 Family-specific design proposals for The Quest

These are proposals, not findings.

- **Parent and child as linked characters:** each pair shares a bond in the story (mentor and apprentice, or siblings-in-arms), so some challenges need both of them.
- **Role reversals:** at least one scene where the child leads and the parent follows, such as the child navigating while the parent carries the map case.
- **Separate then reunite:** a short stretch where parents and children solve parallel halves of a puzzle, then meet to combine their answers.
- **The blessing scene:** at the destination, each parent names a virtue they saw in their child during the journey, using the virtue tokens from Section 23.
- **Per-family pricing:** one price per family unit (for example, up to two adults and two children, with an add-on for extra children). That keeps the focus on the family rather than on headcount.
- **Scouting fit:** Cub Scouts is already family-based, so the den pilot naturally tests the family model.

# Part IX: The Subcontracting Model (MIHLab as Game Master Only)

## 50. The model

MIHLab markets the program and runs the story. Jim acts only as the game master. An established outfitter or program is the operator of record and supplies certified guides, first aid, gear, permits, camping logistics and insurance. This model is common in outdoor travel, and several Christian outfitters below already run custom trips for outside groups such as churches, youth groups and families.

**Two ways to set it up:**

1. **The partner sells, MIHLab adds the story.** Families book and pay the outfitter directly, and MIHLab is hired as a specialty add-on. This has the lightest legal and insurance load for MIHLab.
2. **MIHLab sells, the partner runs the trip.** Families pay MIHLab, which pays the outfitter. MIHLab keeps the brand and the customer relationship, but takes on more liability and may trigger Florida's seller-of-travel law (Section 52).

## 51. Contract checklist

These are items to discuss with an attorney, not legal advice.

- A written agreement that splits the duties. The outfitter handles safety, medical care, routes, permits, staffing ratios and the go/no-go decisions. MIHLab handles the narrative and the game.
- **Guides' safety authority always overrides the story.** The game pauses whenever a guide says so.
- MIHLab is named as an **additional insured** on the outfitter's liability policy, and the outfitter provides a certificate of insurance.
- MIHLab still carries **its own general liability policy**, and possibly professional liability, as the game master.
- Waivers come from the operator, and MIHLab is named in them as well.
- Indemnification runs both ways, each party covering its own negligence.
- Background checks for anyone with direct contact with minors, including Jim (see Section 36).
- Rules on cancellations, refunds, weather and emergencies.
- Ownership of intellectual property: the campaign, characters and materials stay MIHLab's.
- Pricing: a per-family price that combines the outfitter's fee and MIHLab's fee.

## 52. Florida seller-of-travel law

- Florida requires anyone who sells or promotes travel-related services to **register annually with the Florida Department of Agriculture and Consumer Services (FDACS)**, unless exempt.
- Registration for sellers that don't offer vacation certificates takes a **$300 fee and a surety bond of up to $25,000**.
- Nonprofits are **not automatically exempt**. Anyone claiming an exemption must get a letter of exemption from FDACS.
- **Open question:** whether a guided camping trip that MIHLab resells counts as "travel-related services." Model 1 above (families pay the outfitter directly) may avoid the question entirely. Confirm with FDACS or an attorney.

Links: [FDACS Sellers of Travel](https://www.fdacs.gov/Business-Services/Sellers-of-Travel) · [Who must register](https://www.fdacs.gov/Business-Services/Sellers-of-Travel/Who-is-required-to-register) · [Exemptions](https://www.fdacs.gov/Business-Services/Sellers-of-Travel/What-do-sellers-of-travel-claiming-exemption-from-the-registration-requirements-under-Section-559.935-F.S.-need-to-do) · [Nonprofits](https://www.fdacs.gov/Business-Services/Sellers-of-Travel/Are-nonprofit-corporations-exempt-from-registration-under-the-Seller-of-Travel-Law-s.-559.927-F.S) · [Registration package (PDF)](https://flrules.org/gateway/readRefFile.asp?refId=1055&filename=10200+.pdf)

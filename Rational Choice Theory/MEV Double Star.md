A number of philosophers are concerned with normative theories of rational choice, that is, how should people decide to act? Eleonore Stump anticipates an approach that deviates from standard normative theories of rational choice. Professor Stump bases her approach on the narrative self arguing that analytic philosophy can become too narrow and myopic, excluding “complex, nuanced thought, behavior, relations of persons” (Stump 2010, 23). In this account, she distances her approach from standard ones in rational choice theory. I draw from popular narrative thought and attempt a rational decision formula that is philosophically rich and based on a narrative self account. At the center, my formula focuses on the fact that we do not always have the right kind of access to our own mental states. Some decisions require such access however. I argue that a narrative self account of rational choice can allow for this important nuance. How do we decide what to do? Decision theory proposes an answer.

Decision theory derives from a long tradition in consequentialism “If, then, there is some end of things doable in action that we wish for because of itself, and the others because of it” [Nichomachean Ethics Bk I, chapter 2]. However, “doing whatever will provide the most value” has been criticized [See Lewis, 1981]. It is argued that we do not have the necessary access to the world that would allow us to follow through on our plans. This is because we do not have perfect knowledge of outcomes. Orthodox theory, attempts to improve on decision theory. We should do whatever action has the most expected outcome. Decision theory advices that whatever we decide, that when we act, we should “do whatever has the most valuable outcome” (See Brian Skyrms, “Causal Decision Theory,” _The Journal of Philosophy_ 79, no. 11 (1982): 695–711, [https://doi.org/10.2307/2026547.)](https://doi.org/10.2307/2026547.\)) or the outcome with the most expected value.

We will often err when trying to maximize the actual value of an action since we are often uncertain about which acts, out of those available to us, will have the best outcome. There are various reasons why such access is unavailable. Most notably, this kind of evidence is absent when a value state would be experienced by another who to our own mind is significantly different from ourselves. For instance, knowledge of persons “and their mental states” facilitated by the mirror neuronal system [Stump 2010, 68; also see Gallegher 2005]. Therefore, as the objection goes, our imperfect epistemic access to the state of the world imposes limits on our ability to follow Maximize Expected Value” (Carter 2026, 2; cf. Jackson 1991; Howard-Snyder 1997; Lenman 2000). Knowledge of other minds, even our own, is an example of this.

In summary, Orthodox decision theory recommends acting in a way that maximizes actual or expected value. However our credences with regard to what we value, are too opaque for us to be able to identify the acts which maximize actual or expected value (Carter 2026; Williamson 2000;2011, cf. Srinivasan 2015; Lasonen-Aarnio 2019;2024; Lasonen 2025;2026; Hughes 2021). Some decisions require such access however. I first look at recent attempts to save maximize value. I then defend a narrative self account of rational choice.

One specific problem in rational choice theory, is the fact that we are often wrong about our priorities.

We might follow some plan of action, only to find out that the promised outcome is not what we desired after all. Such mistakes in judgment then, stem from failures to recognize our own priorities. In other words, they stem from errors in judgment about ourselves. There are many versions of this concern. For instance, let’s imagine that on the basis of a coinflip, we will either be shown a genuine or a counterfeit Rothko. If we are shown the counterfeit but not the genuine version, we would be less certain about questioning its authenticity, since we would have no reason to question the authenticity of the fake Rothko having no belief about the real one. Therefore we assign equal credence to either possibility, that the one we will bid is real and the one we do not know about is fake or vice versa. Therefore, we might mistakenly think that upon looking at a counterfeit Rothko, that we desire to bid on the counterfeit Rothko rather than a genuine one.

According to some, whether or not these mistakes are avoidable is an open question as it is plausible that a key kind of evidence necessary to make correct judgments about ourselves, is not a part of the set of evidence that we have. In the Rothko case, we would have more reason to question whether one or the other is genuine assuming that we have each of them before us versus if we only see one, then we would have less reason to question whether there is another one that exists somewhere else. The kind of common mistake that we make, which is important for Stump’s thesis, is that an opaque decision problem with respect to decision theory concerns special personal relationships. If we are confronted with two alternative versions of ourselves, then we would have more reason to question whether one, rather than the other, is genuine. $\Pi = S \rightarrow A$ is the set of plans determined by $\hat{\Delta}$.

Plans, $\pi, \pi', \pi'', . . . \in \Pi$ are functions from states $s,$ to acts, i.e., $\pi(s) = a$ is the act $a$ recommended by plan $\pi$ in state $s$.

MEV (Maximize Expected Value). Let $\Pi_{|MEV}$be the set of plans for MEV in $\hat{\Delta}$. Let $ev_{s}(a)$ be the expected value of action $a$ at $s$ (for any $s \in S$ and $s \in A$).

$ev_{s}(a) = \sum\limits_{s’ \in S} \upsilon(a(s’)) \cdot P_{s}(s’)$

Then $\pi \in \Pi_{|MEV}$ iff for all $s \in S$ and $a \in A$:

$ev_{s}(\pi(s)) \geq ev_{s}(a)$

The expected value of an action at some state s, is the sum of values of the outcomes produced by that action at each state.

We weight the sum of values according to the probability that is assigned to that state from the perspective of that state.

A plan to maximize expected value then recommends, in each state, one of the acts which has optimal expected value at that state.

Step 3

The way an agent acts depends on the plan they are trying to follow. However, agents like us who are susceptible to error do not always act according to what their plan recommends for the state they are in. Rather we can understand their susceptibility to err in terms of their underlying pattern of dispositions. To represent these dispositions, we need to represent the state an agent is acting as if they are in, what we can call “enriched state spaces”.

For any opaque decision problem $\hat{\Delta}: [\cdot]$ is a bijective function with domain $S$ which is a product of its domain and codomain, $\bar{S}_{[\cdot]} = S \times \{[s] : s \in S\}$

Where any state $s, [s]$ represents an agent whose act is determined according to what their plan recommends for $s$ and an enriched state, $(s, [s’])$ represents the circumstances of an agent who is in s but whose act is determined according to what their plan recommends for $s’$. Sets of states in the enriched state space can be thought of as propositions which can tell us about:

1. what the world is like
2. what the agent will act as if the world is like

Step 4

Now we musts introduce a state-independent probability function over the enriched state space. This will represent the agent’s credences prior to finding themselves in the decision situation.

**Definition 5 (State-Independent Probability). For any** $\hat{\Delta}$ and $[\cdot]$: a state-independent probability function, $P$, is a probability function over $P(\bar{S}_{[\cdot]})$.

The state-independent probability function takes a stance on both what the world is like and how the agent will act as if the world is like. But it does not incorporate any information the agent acquires once they are at a particular state in the decision problem. For each state $s$, it reflects the agent’s prior credence regarding both how likely they are to find themselves at $s$, their prior dispositions for $s$, and how likely they are to act as if they are at $s$.

Therefore, for any $s,s’$,

$P([s’]|s) =$ $\frac{P((s,[s']))}{P(\{(s,[s'']):s''\in S)\})}$

That the probability that an agent’s estimate of their susceptibility to error at a state, s, relative to a plan, $\pi$ is their credence assuming that they are in $s$, that they will act according to what their plan recommends for $s’$ instead.

According to $P$, an agent’s susceptibility to error at $s$, relative to $\pi$ is proportional to:

$\sum\limits_{\substack{s' \in S \\ \pi(s)\neq\pi(s')}}P([s']|s)$

Eleonore Stump defends a two-part Thomistic account of personal identity. Awe is an epistemic emotion that we experience when we perceive or conceptualize vastness and need to cognitively accommodate our perceptions. One evening, yourself and some friends see an unfamiliar dirt road leading into what you know to be, for you, an unexplored area of the Southern California high desert. It’s late, around 9pm, and this area is dark. You press on. The headlights from the car that you are driving give you an unwarranted sense of security amidst the pitch darkness of the desert around you. The road narrows somewhat and you notice a stark rise in elevation. There are several switchbacks as you continue to gain altitude. Finally the road ends at a small plateau which you ascend and find yourself on level ground. You get out of the car and turn off the car’s lights looking up towards the clear night sky, at which point you have an unobstructed and unpolluted view of the night sky above. There’s nothing to discover, or understand about what lies before you.

Similar to awe, wonder is also an epistemic emotion that we experience when we glimpse at something that is similar but still unknown to us. You are walking along a walking trail near your home when you see for the first time, an uncommon creature such as a snake of unusual hue and coloration. You stand in the same spot for several minutes while you try to think of all you have seen that is closely related to the object in front of you, and all that you might have heard.

Both emotions are important for the mental states related to knowledge. #### Maximize Expected Value

_**MEV**_ characterizes a set of plans $\Pi$ that will maximize expected value in opaque decision problem $\Delta$. $\Pi_{|MEV}$, $ev_s(a)$ is the expected value of $a$ at $s$ for any $s \in S$ and $s \in A$.

#### Definition 3

$$  
ev_s(a) = \sum_{s' \in S} \upsilon(a(s')) \cdot P_s(s')  
$$

We can understand this as a claim about how an agent may attribute value to some given action based on the state that she is in. For instance, in _**Stackin’ Cars**_, the agent is fairly satisfied with her life. However, if the agent is the same agent as the agent in _**Stunted -oul,**_ then it appears that she at some point become loses her satisfaction. _**Stakin’ Cars**_ created a course of action that included a particular plan. This plan was created according to which some action _**a**_ would have an expected value $ev$ given state _**s.**_

Therefore, the expected value of the action given a state is equal to the sum of one of those states in a set of states, and the value of the action performed when the agent is in that state. We multiply this by the probability that that state is one of the states in the set. The expected value of some $act$ $a$ at $state$ $s$ is the sum of the values of the outcomes produced by that act at each state. What may help us to understand this, is to imagine the character in _**Stackin’ Cars**_ trying to decide whether she should pursue the record label contract, or pursue the significant other. She needs to determine what the expected value of each action would be for her in a future state, all while not knowing what this future state will be.

If this is the case, then some plan will maximize expected value though that plan which is in a set of plans $iff$ for all $s \in S$ and $a \in A:$

$ev_s(\pi(s)) \geq ev_s(a)$

Therefore, the expected value of some plan that considers state of the agent _s_, is greater than or equal to the expected value of action _a_ given state _s_. The question has to do with how do we determine the expected value of an action for the agent when the agent is in state _**s**_. What we are expecting to determine is the sum of the values of each of the outcomes produced by an action when the acter is in a given state. We weight this according to an assigned probability that the actor will be in that that state, which tells us the value of the action for that agent when they are in that state. A good plan then would maximize the value expected in each state for one of the actions that have optimal expected value at that state. In summary, the expected value of some state recommended by some plan for that state is greater than the expected value of an action of some state.

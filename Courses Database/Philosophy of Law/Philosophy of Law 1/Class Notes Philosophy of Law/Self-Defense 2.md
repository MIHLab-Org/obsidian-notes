Killing and harming others are paradigmatic wrongs. And yet there is at least one intuitive exception to this prohibition—namely, killing or harming in self-defense, or in defense of others. Consider:

*Murder*: Attacker is culpably trying to kill Victim because he is jealous of Victim’s success. Victim can save his own life only by lethally throwing a grenade at Attacker.

With the exception of strict pacifists, there is broad consensus in morality and law that defensive harm can be permissible in cases like this. However, as we shall see, it is surprisingly difficult to explain the grounds and limits of this permission.

---

## [1. Liability Justifications](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/self-defense/#LiabJust)

---

On a standard view, the moral wrongness of killing and injuring is grounded in persons’ having stringent moral rights against such treatment

However . . .

*Trolley*: A runaway trolley is lethally heading towards five innocent people. A bystander, Engineer, can divert the trolley away from the five down a side-track. The diverted trolley will kill Workman. (Thomson 1985. Based on a case in Foot 1967)

Why?
    The permission to kill Attacker cannot be explained by his right’s being overridden by the greater good, since killing him does not save more lives. Moreover, Attacker has no standing to complain about being harmed, nor a claim to compensation *ex post* (and he certainly does not have a permission to fight back.) Instead, the permission to kill Attacker is explained by his *lack* of a right not to be killed in the circumstances. This is known as a ***liability***** **justification for harming.

## Liability Justification

- [1.1 Liability vs. Lesser-Evil Justifications](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/self-defense/#LiabVsLessEvilJust)

What does liability to harm mean?

- *forfeiture* of one’s usual rights
-  our rights are already specified in such a way as to accommodate liability: 
	- Attacker does not forfeit his right when he attacks Victim; rather, he never had a right not to be killed under those circumstances (Draper 2016: 92).
- Not limited to forfeiture: identifies costs that we lack rights against bearing quite generally. 
	- A person’s being liable to pay her taxes, for example, does not seem to depend on her having forfeited her usual right to keep all of her income (Tadros 2016a: 110–118).
- 3 [1.2 Grounds of Liability](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/self-defense/#GrouLiab)

What are the conditions under which someone is liable?

---

### [1.2.1 The culpability (blame) account](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/self-defense/#CulpAcco)

The relevance (if any) of facts about individuals’ *agency,* such as their beliefs, evidence, intentions, culpability, and degree of moral responsibility.
    *Conscientious Driver*: Driver, who always drives carefully and keeps her car well-maintained, faultlessly loses control of her car. She will hit and kill Victim unless Victim blows up the car, and Driver, with a grenade (McMahan 2005a: 393–394). (Driver is usually described as a (minimally) *morally responsible threatener*.)
        **Driver is not liable because she acts permissibly given her evidence, despite the risk she poses, and so is not culpable.**
    *Ray Gun*: Falling Person is helplessly blown by the wind down a well, at the bottom of which Victim is trapped. Falling Person will crush Victim to death unless Victim vaporizes her with his ray gun. If he does not vaporize her, Falling Person will survive her fall (Nozick 1974: 34). (Falling Person is typically described as an *innocent* or *non-responsible threatener*.)
        **Falling Person is not liable because she is not acting, and thus cannot be acting culpably.**

---

### [1.2.2 The causal account](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/self-defense/#CausAcco)

 *causal* relations between an individual and a threat of harm.
     It holds that there is a single explanation of the permissibility of harming both culpable threateners, such as Attacker, and innocent threateners, such as Falling Person—namely, that each will violate Victim’s right not to be harmed unless Victim harms them (Thomson 1991).
    Why?
    One does not need to exercise agency in order to violate a right. 
    For Example:
        if I forget to meet you for lunch, despite promising to do so, I fail in my duty to keep my promise even if my forgetting is not a result of my agency, and I have no control over my forgetting.
        **What about?**
        if I get into a car accident after promising to meet you for lunch, despite promising to do so, I fail in my duty to keep my promise even if my forgetting is not a result of my agency, and I have no control over my forgetting.
    However, Thomson’s reliance on causal roles makes it hard for her to prohibit harming at least some innocent people whom she herself considers it impermissible to harm. **(Bombing a munitions factory next to a primary school?)**

---

### [1.2.3 The responsibility account](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/self-defense/#RespAcco)

- The *responsibility account* holds that a person is liable for posing an unjust threat only if they are morally responsible for doing so (McMahan 1994, 2005a; Otsuka 1994; 2016).
	- What is responsibility?
		- (1) of sound mind, 
		- (2) in control of her actions, and 
		- (3) aware of the dangerousness of what she is doing. (Otsuka 2016: 52)

But a person might be morally responsible without being culpable (worthy of blame)
    *Mistake*: Homeowner sees on the news that a dangerous murderer has escaped from the local prison. Victim, the murderer’s innocent identical twin, breaks down in his car near Homeowner’s house. Unaware of his brother’s escape, Victim rings the doorbell, intending to ask to use the phone. Homeowner mistakes Victim for his murderous brother, and takes aim at Victim with her shotgun. (McMahan 2005a: 387)

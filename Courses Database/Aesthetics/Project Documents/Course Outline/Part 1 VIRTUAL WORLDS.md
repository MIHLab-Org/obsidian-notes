Three Questions
    Can we know whether or not we’re in a virtual world?
> We can never prove we’re not in a computer simulation because any evidence of ordinary reality—whether the grandeur of nature, the antics of your cat, or the behavior of other people—could presumably be simulated.

    Are virtual worlds real or illusory?
> Here’s what I think: Simulations are not illusions. Virtual worlds are real. Virtual objects really exist.

    Can you lead a good life in a virtual world?
> Certainly virtual worlds can be dystopian, just as the physical world can be, but they won’t be dystopian merely because they’re virtual. As with most technologies, whether VR is good or bad depends entirely on how it’s used.

## True Philosophical Reflection

> Our charge against the metaphysician is not that be attempts to employ the understanding in a field where it cannot profitably venture, but that he produces sentences which fail to conform to the conditions under which alone a sentence can be literally significant Nor are we ourselves obliged to talk nonsense in order to show that all sentences of a certain type are necessarily devoid of literal significance. [[Language, Truth and Logic]] 

A common misunderstanding is that philosophers make meaningless statements.

Therefore:

- We need only formulate the criterion which enables us to test whether a sentence expresses a genuine proposition about a matter of fact, and then point out that the sentences under consideration fail to satisfy it.
- We shall first of all formulate the criterion in somewhat vague terms, and then give the explanations which are necessary to render it precise.

> We say that a sentence is factually significant to any given person, if, and only if, he knows how to verify the proposition which it purports to express—that is, if he knows what observations would lead him, under certain conditions, to accept the proposition as being true, or reject it as being false.

- In order for a sentence to be meaningful
	- It must be a proposition, a statement that is either true or false
	- Utterer then asks “under what conditions is the statement true or false?”

> There are extraterrestrial lifeforms

- Practical verifiability
	- I can prove the claim
- Verifiability in Principle
	- I know what it would take to prove the claim

- **Strong**: A proposition is said to be verifiable, in the strong sense of the term, if. and only if, its truth could be conclusively established in experience.
- **Weak**: But it is verifiable. in the weak sense, if it is possible for experience to render it probable.

> As a result, simulations are not just a guide to our actual universe. They are also a guide to the vast cosmos of possible universes. Philosophers call these possible worlds.

- Philosophers generally use the term ‘thought experiment’ fairly loosely to refer to hypothetical scenarios that are used to challenge or substantiate a theory.
	- First, thought experiments can be used to challenge a theory by identifying a difficult case that it cannot answer.
	- Second, thought experiments can model situations that may be untestable using empirical methods.
	- Finally, thought experiments are much less dangerous than real-world experiments, both in the senses of their risk of physical harm and their moral harm.

## About the Course

In the edited volume *Thought Experiments in Science and Philosophy*, Tamara Horowitz and Gerald Massey define thought experiments as "attempt[s] to draw instruction from a process of hypothetical reasoning". 

Planning and imagination play an important role in many of the activities that we carry out on a daily basis. 

Some of these activities are associated with escape, daydreaming or fantasy. However, some of the activities that imagination play an important role in are associated with learning, and enable us to learn about not only about the world as it is, but also ourselves.

For instance, when we wake up in the morning, we imagine the weather. We say to ourselves, if it is raining outside, and I go out, then I will get wet. Here, we imagine the weather, we then imagine some consequence of the weather, if I go out, then I will get wet, and we ask ourselves how we might feel about that. I will get wet, I will not like being wet. We then make some kind of plan in response to the way we might feel. So here, we have an imagination, imagining that it is raining, then we imagine a consequence of that, I will get wet,  then we imagine a further consequence, I will not like being wet. So we make a plan, I will bring an umbrella. 

As it turns out, thought experiments facilitate planning and imagination and as such, thought experiments are important for learning, i.e., they are a process of hypothetical reasoning that are useful to draw instruction from.

Video games can and often do, function as thought experiments. They can at one time be used to escape or daydream, but they can also be used to help us learn about the world as it is, for instance when we use them to model problems in metaphysics, values, epistemology, or the hard sciences.

This course is built around the idea that by treating video games as a philosophical thought experiment, you and I can work alongside one another to promote philosophical reflection and moral learning in the classroom, in this space. Helen De Cruz in fact got a group of prominent philosophers together to narratively write philosophical thought experiments in an edited work called "Philosophy through science fiction stories." I think that she would love what we're doing here. Unfortunately she passed away from cancer last year. She would never get to see what we are aiming for here.

Before I move on, I should point out that I am equating “moral learning” with “philosophical reflection”. In doing this, I am claiming that to think deeply about something is an act of moral virtue, to wrestle with, to rigorously test, to fight with, both in the abstract and the subjective, and that to truly experience an idea, cultivates virtue in the learner.

This is not a completely novel idea however, but an emerging one. We'll talk more about this as the semester progresses.

So how will this course progress? We will first use course readings to design these game play experiences to be carried out inside open world simulator type games, we will then use these gameplay experiences to reflect on and discuss course readings.

What Roberts is suggesting, is a closer relationship between a player’s in-game character and their own personal identity. This point is similar to questions about consciousness and mind. Gabriel Marcel has also argued that when we lose our sense of wonder, our sense of what defines us is lost, our humanity. We begin to only see ourselves as a set of functions and sources of productivity, where similar to Robert’s claim, death is only a financial and time inconvenience. In other words, we begin to lose our ability to identity with our in-game characters in much the same way that we begin to lose our ability to identify with ourselves in real-life.

One argument is that the scientific method, by its very nature is central to our our loss of wonder, namely that the purpose of science is to dispel our sense of wonder by removing objects of mystery, for instance cancer. If this is true, then science by its very nature, is responsible for our loss of wonder. 

Conversely however, Helen De Cruz argues that this story about science is incomplete. Rather, the insights of science help us to discover new sources of wonder. Not having to worry about our loved ones dying of cancer frees us up to give our attention to other more meaningful things. This sounds plausible. First our emotional systems, and therefore our emotions are central to the way that our minds work [[The emotional mind]]. Given that the evolution of the way our brain works, then our emotions, a faculty of our mind, is central to how organisms like us take care of our offspring and avoid potentially harmful situations. As such, it ought to be the case that our emotions can arise to new sources of wonder, objects that science reveals for us.

This is related to the death mechanic as it would seem that if science does dispel our sense of wonder, then one way it might do so, is by extending our mortal lives so that death is something we need to worry about less. For instance, a future wherein humans can merely regenerate their bodies and download their consciousness into newly cloned bodies, is not one that would make our actions meaningless strengthening the claim that science reduces our humanity. Rather, such a future could be one where a person devotes hundreds of years to exploring the cosmos.

A primary component of this course is to run De Cruz’s idea as a narrative thought experiment. This course will use this thought experiment to test these two positions by contrasting them against one another. For instance, looking at death mechanics in video games, does the existence of this mechanic mean that the player is more committed to satisfying their wonder and curiosity within the game? [ ]

Along the way, the course broadly uses questions about the love, wonder, meaning and other concepts of mind to ask about mind, consciousness, value and meaning. It is a joint course that doubles either as an introduction philosophy, or to the philosophy of mind, organized around a sustained inquiry into love, emotion, suffering, and meaning, all thought to be aspects of wonder.  We will cover topics such as philosophical materialism, intentionality, consciousness, other minds, and the self. All these topics will be covered in contrast to the primary question about whether games should remove the death mechanic or keep it in.

As a philosophy of mind course, each week opens with a short reading from Timothy Bayne’s *Philosophy of Mind: An Introduction* (2021) that establishes the standard philosophical problem under discussion. The other primary texts — Brigitte Brogaard’s *On Romantic Love*, Martha Nussbaum’s *Love’s Knowledge*, together with excerpts from her *Fragility of Goodness*, and Eleonore Stump’s *Wandering in Darkness* — are then read as extended engagements with that problem. Students will see that love, wonder, meaning, are not peripheral topics in the philosophy of mind, but that the philosophy of mind and an introduction to philosophy are courses where our deepest questions, where we look for questions science has not yet solved, and try to understand what would be needed to solve these questions. In using video games to model questions about physicalism, intentionality, consciousness, other minds, and the self — these questions become unusually vivid and philosophically tractable.

### The Experience Machine

These answers reflect two different answers to the Value Question: Could you live a good life in virtual reality? Why wouldn’t we? We want to be importantly connected to reality, not to live in a delusion. But what does it mean to say “importantly connected to reality?” What is important? What is important about it? In the experience machine, we’re not courageous or kind; we’re not any sort of person. We’re just indeterminate blobs. In the experience machine, we’re limited to a human-made reality. Everything we experience was constructed by humans.

No chance? No uncertainty?

First, you don’t know you’re in the experience machine while you’re in there, but you know you’re in the reality machine. How might you not know? Because of the lack of chance and risk? How important is chance and risk? Second, the experience machine preprograms all your experiences, but the reality machine does not. The reality machine allows your friends and family to share a reality with you.

Is VR illusory?

In his 2000 Forbes article extending the experience machine to real-life VR, he said that the contents of VR are not “truly real.” VR is not preprogrammed. Typically, it’s open-ended. So users can indeed be genuinely courageous or genuinely kind in the reality machine.

“Which option would be best for me?”

But even when we speak of what’s best for others, it’s a matter of personal value: What’s better or worse for them? In this chapter, when I ask what makes for a good life, I’m asking: What makes a life good for oneself?

To ask what makes a good life for someone, perhaps we can start by asking what makes anything good for someone. **hedonism:** something is good for someone to the extent that it gives them pleasure and not pain.

- The frivolous pleasure from a parlor game counts as much as the high-minded pleasure from cultural pursuits.
	- wireheading: users experience great pleasure all the time.

**experientialism:** the fundamental objects of value are conscious experiences.

- Experientialism says that something is good for someone to the extent that it gives them positive experiences and not negative experiences.

The experience machine goes beyond the pleasure machine by supplying the user not just with pleasure but with experiences of all sorts. Nozick makes the case that inside the experience machine, you’ll have more positive experiences in the experience machine than you will have outside the experience machine—but nevertheless it’s preferable to live outside the machine than to live inside the machine. It follows that experientialism is incorrect; experiences aren’t all that matter.

**desire-satisfaction:** a good life is one in which our desires are satisfied, or in which things are as we want them to be.

- Our life goes better when the world is as we want it to be, even if this doesn’t affect our own experiences.

Hedonism says that a life with the unfaithful partner is just as good as a life with the faithful partner, because the experiences are the same. In this case, we want our partner to be faithful. On the desire-satisfaction view, value is largely subjective, but the **social view of value:** where the core idea here is that all value comes from our connections to other people.

Finally, there’s the **objective-list view of value**, in which the basic source of value are the items on a list: say, **knowledge, friendship, fulfillment**, and more. Some will say that sheer physicality is missing in VR, and physicality is something we value.

### Works of Art

David Chalmers gives also gives an argument for why the Matrix question is a difficult question to solve. If you are unfamiliar with this question, it is just the skeptical hypothesis that we are living in a simulation. He answers on two fronts, one, that we cannot know that we are not living in a simulation, and two, that even if we were living in a simulation, what would it matter? Here is the brief argument:

1.  It’s not ***impossible ***that we are living in a simulation. (Metaphysical Thesis)
2. Therefore we cannot know that we are not living in a simulation. (Skeptical Epistemology)
3. As such, why would it matter if we were living in a simulation? (Anti-Skeptical Value Theory)

Importantly, his argument is not so much a defense of why we should think that we are living in a simulation, but why the question is one without a possible answer and why the answer may not even be that interesting.  Notably, this book really is a broader explanation about what philosophers do, and why they’re jobs are interestingly important. 

Covering all the major areas of philosophy, metaphysics, epistemology, value theory, logic, what Chalmers does is introduce the novice reader to the philosophical discipline. But Let’s imagine that Chalmers himself takes not the primary thesis as fundamental, not the negative thesis that we cannot know, but the positive thesis, that if we are living in a simulation, then what? 

One important claim that he makes, is that if we are living in a simulation, then the objects around us, though virtual, are real, they are just really virtual.

## Outline of Kant's Foundation for a Metaphysics of Morals

1. "What contribution does pure reason make to our knowledge of the world and to the government of our actions?" (Grounding, introduction). The debate with which Kant is initially involved in takes place between those who would consider themselves rationalists and those who would call themselves empiricists. The empiricists argued that all of our knowledge, as well as "our moral ideas" is the result of our experiences in the world. For instance, our knowledge that 2 + 2 = 4 is derived from experience involving our five senses. As such, knowledge that it is wrong to lie, is a matter of experience, but there are no such experiences available to us, and therefore it does not make sense to say that it is wrong to lie. Section 2.1 of [the sep article on Kant](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kant/#KanProThePurRea) would point out that this was a response to the enlightenment.^[A time when new discoveries in science would provide new empirical understanding of the way the world is organized. At such a time when empirical investigation contributes to so much of our understanding of the world, Kant defends rationalism. Rationalists "believed that . . . all truths could be derived from self-evident rational principles" [@kant98a, viii]. This view is extreme, but there are other views more  commonly shared among rationalists such as the existence of God or the soul.
]
2. Moral judgments are judgments such as "it is wrong to lie", or "one should not cheat on academic tests." Kant uses the concept of judgments to make a distinction between rational and empirical kinds of knowledge. Two distinctions apply to judgments. Analytic/synthetic concerns what makes a judgment true or false. "A judgment is analytic if the predicate is contained in the concept of the subject (true by definition, explains the meaning of the term, for instance a square has 4 sides is true because having 4 sides is how we define a square); otherwise, the predicate adds something new to our conception of the subject and the judgment is synthetic" [@kant98a, viii]. 2) A priori/a posteriori distinction. Concerns the way we know that a judgment is true. A judgment = a posteriori if it is known from experience. A priori if our knowledge of it is independent of any particular experience.
3. Placing the two distinctions together yields three possible types of judgment.^[	1. Judgment = analytically true then known a priori "we do not need experience to tell us what is contained in our concepts" [@kant98a, ix].
	2. synthetically true = a posteriori because subject and predicate our conjoined in our conceptions.
	3. synthetic a priori = tells us something new but independent of experience
]
4. Can pure reason guide us (e.g., metaphysical speculation "does God exist", or action "should I lie")? Amounts to a question whether "and how  we can establish any synthetic *a priori* judgments" [@kant98a, ix]. The question, not whether we have  moral obligations (seems that we do), but rather, where do this obligations come from? The answer, must be discovered synthetically (sense it is new knowledge), *a priori* sense it is from reason and not experience. It is  not whether or not we have moral obligations, but rather, how do we prove that we have moral obligations?

## Section 1 

What is common about morally good actions? This sort of question: "to proceed analytically from common cognition to the determination of its supreme principle" [AK 4: 392]. To discover the answer, we first have to understand how we generally think about such questions. "start from our ordinary ways of thinking about morality and analyze them to discover the principle behind them" [xi].^[The present treatise is, however, nothing more than the investigation and establishment of the supreme principle of morality, and this alone constitutes a study complete in itself and one which ought to be kept apart from every other moral investigation. No doubt my conclusions on this weighty question, which has hitherto been very unsatisfactorily examined, would receive much light from the application of the same principle to the whole system, and would be greatly confirmed by the adequacy which it exhibits throughout; but I must forego this advantage, which indeed would be after all more gratifying than useful, since the easy applicability of a principle and its apparent adequacy give no very certain proof of its soundness, but rather inspire a certain partiality, which prevents us from examining and estimating it strictly in itself and without regard to consequences. So, what values do such actions (morally good ones) have? Kant argues that the value in such actions rest in the will. This is because a good will is good unconditionally.^["A good will is good not because of what it performs or effects, not by its aptness for the attainment of some proposed end, but simply by virtue of the volition; that is, it is good in itself, and considered by itself is to be esteemed much higher than all that can be brought about by it in favour of any inclination, nay even of the sum total of all inclinations

## Some Examples

* A brilliant scientist  may use their gifts for evil work.
* A political leader may achieve good results using ruthless methods
* A wealthy aesthete may lead a generous and happy life donating to institutions that preserve cultural treasures, but may ignore the lives of the less fortunate.
* A wealthy tech guru may advance science and technology but ignore humanity

Regardless of the results, the intentions of the agent are unaffected by the results.

Therefore the motivation of the agent is what gives an action its *moral* value. "This implies that once we know how actions with unconditional value are willed---once we know what principle a person like the rescuer acts on---we will know what makes them morally good" [@kant98a, xii].

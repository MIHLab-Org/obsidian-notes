## Experiment 1 — Death and Permadeath in Elite Dangerous

**Window: Complete anytime July 13-19. Due on Canvas**

**Philosophical Question**
Does the possibility of permanent, irreversible loss make virtual
experience more meaningful? If stakes were real — if death in the game
meant losing your progress permanently — would the experience feel
more genuine?

**Background**
Before running this experiment, complete the Week 1 and Week 2 readings
(Chalmers, Chs. 1–7). Both argue that virtual experience is genuine. We
will test that claim by introducing genuine stakes.

**Procedure**

*Part A — Standard death:* Accept a medium-difficulty mission (combat
or exploration). At some point during the mission, allow your ship to be
destroyed. Do not resist. Observe the respawn sequence. Note what you feel.

*Part B — Reflective permadeath:* Accept another mission of similar
difficulty. Before starting, commit to the following rule: *if I die, I
will delete my save and start over.* You do not have to actually delete
your save — but make the commitment genuine in your imagination. Attempt
the mission. Note what changes.

*Part C — The thought experiment:* Now consider: what if your grade in
this course depended on your commander surviving the semester? Would the
experience feel different? How?

**What to Notice**
Did your behavior change between Part A and Part B? Did your emotional
state change? Did the virtual world feel more or less real when genuine
loss was possible?

## Experiment 2 — Extended Cognition and Atmospheric Entry

**Window: Complete anytime July 11–17. Bring your log to class July 14.**

**Philosophical Question**
Does the HUD (heads-up display) extend your mind into the ship — or
does it replace your mind? When you remove it, are you more or less
present in the virtual world?

**Background**
Andy Clark and David Chalmers argued in "The Extended Mind" (1998) that
cognitive processes can extend beyond the brain into tools and
environments. The HUD in Elite Dangerous is a cognitive tool — it
provides altitude, speed, heat, and structural integrity in real time.
Without it, you must navigate by sight, sensation, and judgment alone.

This experiment uses atmospheric entry (planetary landing approach) as
the test environment because it requires precise attention to altitude
and angle — exactly the kind of task the HUD is designed to support.

**Procedure**

*Part A — HUD enabled:* Enter glide toward a planet's surface with the
HUD fully active. Navigate the approach. Note what information you are
attending to and how you are making decisions.

*Part B — HUD off:* Enter glide again, this time disabling the HUD
(Options → Graphics → HUD). Navigate by visual cues alone. Note what
changes in your attention, your decision-making, your sense of presence.

*Part C — Reflection:* Which approach felt more like you were flying the
ship? Which felt more like the ship was flying itself?

**Safety Note**
Entering glide without the HUD is difficult. You may crash. That is fine.
Crashing is data.

**Write-Up Template**

Use Chalmers' language and concepts. Your write-up demonstrates your
understanding of the extended mind thesis (Ch. 16).

*Observation: What intuition are you testing? (e.g., Clark and Chalmers
say the mind can extend into tools — but does it feel that way?)*

*Hypothesis: "I claim that X." Example: "The HUD functions as an
extended cognitive system — removing it reveals which cognitive tasks
the player has genuinely internalized."*

*Materials: HUD options in Elite Dangerous; planetary landing approach;
glide mechanics.*

*Method:*

| Phase | Elite Dangerous |
| --- | --- |
| Baseline (Part A — HUD on) | Complete a Task with the HUD |
| Extended Case (Part B — HUD off) | Remove the HUD |
| Contra-Positive (Part C — Reflection) | Remove the HUD during the task. |

*Results: What did each phase reveal?*

*Conclusion: Did the experiment support the extended mind thesis? What
does this mean for your argument?*

## Experiment 3 — Risk, Respawn, and Moral Emotions

**Window: Complete anytime July 25–31. Bring your log to class July 28.**

**Philosophical Question**
Does the structure of risk in a game determine whether the emotions it
produces are genuine forms of knowledge? Do low-stakes games produce
shallow emotions while high-stakes games produce genuine ones?

**Background**
Nussbaum argues that emotions are appropriate when they track something
real and valuable. A trivial loss produces a trivial emotion. A genuine
loss produces genuine grief. We will test this by manipulating the
stakes of gameplay.

**Procedure — Four Conditions**

| Condition | Mission Risk | Respawn | What to Notice |

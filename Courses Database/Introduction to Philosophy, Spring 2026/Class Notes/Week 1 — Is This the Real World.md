- Chalmers: 
	Last time I taught this course, we focused very heavily on David Chalmers’ book Reality+. This was definitely a great experience, and I myself enjoyed it immensely. Initially I was going to teach the same material for a second time, and to be fair, I will recycle quite a bit of the material, however I think that we would all be better served if we looked more closely at some of the source material Chalmers is working from. Namely, teaching this course last semester has made me question other concepts that I thought I was solid on, especially the Christian concept of redemption and whether respawn mechanics in open world games can teach us anything about it. It should be mentioned now that I am not using the term redemption in the spooky metaphysical sense, but in the sense that anyone can relate to, the sense of deep meaning in life. It is perhaps the case that this sense can lead to the spooky metaphysical sense, but it’s important to note that before that happens, a person has to care deeply first about ultimate truth and reality. So, what I mean to say is this, that some of this additional material will make up the foundation of my course notes, but this interplay between salvific redemption in the sense of deep meaning and curiosity and death mechanics is central. 
	But, in the same way that you all are taking a course to learn about the discipline of philosophy, I too am in a course of sorts. With each class I instruct, I learn more about people and about myself. I hope that as you all continue on with whatever career you choose, or discipline you choose to follow, that you will continue to learn in it as well. Learning often starts with questions in this sense of salvific redemption or deep meaning and curiosity. Sometimes those questions are spontaneous. But sometimes they are not, sometimes they are the natural development of an event, activity and what one truly cares about, and the natural progression of what comes after. Speaking about the problem of consciousness, Chalmers’ himself describes just such a progression, deep meaning, care, and curiosity in the fifteenth chapter of this book:
> My academic background was in mathematics and physics: I did a first degree in mathematics in Australia in the 1980s and got partway through a doctorate at Oxford. I loved those fields because they seemed to address truly fundamental questions. But it gradually came to seem to me that most of the really hard questions had been answered, and the fundamentals were pretty well understood (or at least so I supposed at the time). Perhaps that was wrong, but that was how things seemed to me then. At the same time, there seemed to be a truly fundamental problem as yet wide open: the problem of consciousness. The human mind seemed to pose many of the harder questions left for science, and of these, consciousness seemed the hardest of all. Consciousness was the most familiar thing in the world—but also the thing we understood the least. How did it fit into the physical world? How could there be subjective experience in an objective world? No one knew. I became obsessed by these questions, to the point where I decided to leave mathematics and work on the problem of consciousness directly.
    For Chalmers, this activity was the pursuit of formal mathematics, and the natural progression for him was philosophy by way of consciousness. His pursuit began with deep meaning and care, curiosity. While I do not think that we will get to the most fundamental questions, life, existence, relationship, in this course, I think that we will look at some really intriguing ones and be able to ask ourselves, what intuitions do we share, on which do we diverge, and most importantly, why? Most importantly however, I hope that through this course, you too discover your own sources of deep meaning, curiosity and care.

    # Welcome to Playing at the REAL Good Life
    ## Course Theme: Otherworldly Redemption — Virtual Love and Real Sanctification
    Philosophy begins with wonder. This course begins with a simple question: *can a virtual world be a genuine site of meaning, presence, and the good life?*
    We read philosophers who take seriously questions about consciousness, presence, vulnerability, and flourishing. Then — for three of our sessions — we will test those ideas the only way that matters: by actually inhabiting them. Our experimental environment is **Elite Dangerous**, a persistent-universe space simulation.
    You do not need to be a gamer to succeed in this course. You need to be willing to think carefully about your experience.

# Is this the real life?

- Zhuangzi’s butterfly dream (knowledge/epistemology)
- Narada’s Transformation (existence/reality/metaphysics)
- Plato’s Cave (value/value theory)

### Epistemology

> Once Zhuangzi dreamt he was a butterfly, a butterfly flitting and fluttering around, happy with himself and doing as he pleased. He didn’t know he was Zhuangzi. Suddenly he woke up and there he was, solid and unmistakably Zhuangzi. But he didn’t know if he was Zhuangzi who had dreamt he was a butterfly, or a butterfly dreaming he was Zhuangzi.

### Metaphysics

> Roy: A Life Well Lived. (It would be even better if Morty had played Sue: A Life Well Lived, but you can’t have everything.) Morty lives Roy’s entire fifty-five-year life: childhood, football star, carpet salesman, cancer patient, death. When he emerges from the game a moment later as Morty, his grandfather berates him for having made the wrong life decisions in the simulation. This is a recurring theme in the series. Its characters are in apparently normal situations that turn out to be simulations and are often led to ask whether their current reality might be a simulation, too.

### Value Theory

> Socrates: Do you think the one who had gotten out of the cave would still envy those within the cave and would want to compete with them who are esteemed and who have power? Or would not he much rather wish for the condition that Homer speaks of, namely “to live on the land [above ground] as the paid menial of another destitute peasant”? Wouldn’t he prefer to put up with absolutely anything else rather than associate with those opinions that hold in the cave and be that kind of human being? Glaucon: I think that he would prefer to endure everything rather than be that kind of human being.

## The Mapping

How can Zhuangzi know whether or not he’s dreaming? $\rightarrow$ How can Zhuangzi know whether or not he’s dreaming?

Is Narada’s transformation real or illusory? $\rightarrow$ Are virtual worlds real or illusory?

Can one lead a good life in Plato’s cave? $\rightarrow$ Can you lead a good life in a virtual world?

## Three Commitments

- Simulations are illusions
- Virtual worlds are real
- Virtual objects exist

We often learn about Plato by reading his ***Republic***. Plato was born around 429 B.C.E and died in 347 B.C.E.. He was a Greek of the city of Athens and was of particularly high status. He is largely known as a student of Socrates who himself never wrote anything down. Something that I often like to mention every class, is the fact that Socrates intentionally sought to distance himself from the other influential public speakers of his time. There is a tradition in Athenian society, where young men from well to do families, would follow some influential person throughout the square and would eventually become known as a mentee of that person. Socrates himself did not necessarily attempt to portray himself as an influential member of society, he is not know for writing any notable acts of law, or serving in any influential public positions. However, he was known for questioning those who did, and it is for this reason, that young men like Plato followed him around.

Plato is associated with quite a few central doctrines. For instance, that the world, as an object of our perceptions is distorted in some way.

> He will require to grow accustomed to the sight of the upper world. And first he will see the shadows best, next the reflections of men and other objects in the water, and then the objects themselves; then he will gaze upon the light of the moon and the stars and the spangled heaven; and he will see the sky and the stars by night better than the sun or the light of the sun by day?

 In fact, Plato’s cave, which Chalmers notes as a question about value, “should we return to the cave, can we live a valuable life in a simulation?”, is typically referenced as a question about what is real and what is not. In the allegory, a prisoner who has lived their entire lives staring at shadows cast onto a wall in front of them, escapes and realizes for the first time that the shadows are merely poor representations of objects outside of the cave. 

> 
> This entire allegory, I said, you may now append, dear Glaucon, to the previous argument; the prison-house is the world of sight, the light of the fire is the sun, and you will not misapprehend me if you interpret the journey upwards to be the ascent of the soul into the intellectual world according to my poor belief, which, at your desire, I have expressed whether rightly or wrongly God knows. But, whether true or false, my opinion is that in the world of knowledge the idea of good appears last of all, and is seen only with an effort; and, when seen, is also inferred to be the universal author of all things beautiful and right, parent of light and of the lord of light in this visible world, and the immediate source of reason and truth in the intellectual; and that this is the power upon which he who would act rationally, either in public or private life must have his eye fixed.

In Plato’s language, how do we know what a leaf, for instance, is, when there are no two leaves that are the same? Often called his theory of the forms, the argument is intended to make us question what is the nature of the objects of our perceptions? But Plato has us imagine that were we to see these things, the real objects and not their “likenesses”.

## JTB

> A common view of knowledge, going back to Plato, is that knowledge is justified, true belief. To know something, you have to think it’s true (that’s belief), you have to be right about it (that’s truth), and you have to have good reasons for believing it (that’s justification).

The theory of knowledge going back to Plato’s time, was that in order for a person to have knowledge, to know something, three things would first have to occur:

1. The thing known (we can substitute for acceptance) is true
	1. It seems obvious (but nothing ever is?), that what is not true, cannot be known.
		1. My sister lied to me last night and said that she was playing Call of Duty when in fact she was playing Animal Crossing.
		2. I told my parents that my sister was playing Call of Duty last night.
		3. Our aunt called and asked what my sister was doing last night and my parents said “She was playing Call of Duty, ask Professor Reynolds, he knows”.
		4. But I do not in fact know.
2. The knower has to believe (or just needs adequate evidence) that the thing known is true
	2. I told my parents that I know who Santa Clause is.
	3. But I do not believe in Santa Clause.
	4. So I cannot know who Santa Clause is.
3. The knower needs to be justified (just needs to be sure) in believing that the thing known is true

## Skepticism

Let’s look more closely at the second premise above. In order for knowledge to be obtained, a knower has to believe that the thing known is true. But what do we call someone who does not believe that something is true? For instance, Chalmers does not believe that Hillary Clinton ran a child sex ring. This would make Chalmers a skeptic. There are many versions of skepticism and they can be disambiguated into two classes, local and global. Chalmers doubting that Hilary Clinton runs a child sex ring is an extremely local skepticism since it involves skepticism of one particular idea. Both global and local skepticism vary in degrees. This is why Chalmers’ skepticism is a *very *local skepticism. But we can broaden the scope of Chalmers’ skepticism somewhat. To say that something has scope, just means that we would place more objects into it’s category. For instance, we could argue that the reason Chalmers does not believe that Hilary Clinton runs a child sex ring, is because he does not believe that any former 1st ladies run a child sex ring. Hilary Clinton is a former first lady, therefore Hilary Clinton does not run a child sex ring. There are also different kinds of skepticism. There are some skeptics, for instance, who do not believe in moral truths. These are individuals who do not believe that it would be accurate to say “lying is morally wrong”. These people are called moral skeptics.

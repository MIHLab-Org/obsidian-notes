> **Theme:** Is God a Hacker in the Next Universe Up?

## 🎯 Big Questions This Week

- What is God?
- If we are in a simulation, is the creator of that simulation a kind of God?
- What is the relationship between the simulation hypothesis and theology?

---

## 🧠 What is God?

| Classical Attribute | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Creator** | Brought the universe into existence |
| **Omnipotent** | All powerful |
| **Omniscient** | All knowing |
| **Omnibenevolent** | All good |

## 🔬 What About the Hacker?

If we are in a simulation, the entity that created it:

- **Created** our universe — ✓
- Has power **over** our universe — ✓ (can alter parameters, pause, delete)
- May **know** everything in our universe — ✓ (has access to all data)
- May **not** be all good — ? (could be indifferent, malicious, or simply curious)

**Discussion:** Does the simulator satisfy the classical criteria for God? Which criteria does it meet? Which does it fail?

---

## ⚖️ The Simulation Hypothesis as Theology

| Question | Implication |
| --- | --- |
| Is the universe made of information? | The it-from-bit thesis suggests physical reality is grounded in information |
| Is God a hacker in the next universe up? | The simulator has god-like powers but may lack god-like goodness |
| Did simulation create its from bits? | If so, what created the simulator’s universe? |

**Discussion Questions:**

- If the simulation hypothesis is true, does it make religious belief more or less plausible?
- Does the simulator have moral obligations toward us?
- Can there be infinite regress of simulations? What would that mean for the concept of a ‘base reality’?

## 📝 Instructor Notes

> [!note] 👨‍🏫
> This is a naturally engaging week because it connects simulation theory to theology and popular culture (the Matrix, etc.). The key philosophical question is whether the classical attributes of God — omnipotence, omniscience, omnibenevolence, creator — are satisfied by a simulator. Students typically agree the first three are partially met but the fourth is the sticking point. Chalmers is careful to note the simulation hypothesis is not a skeptical hypothesis — it doesn’t say our beliefs are false, only that their grounding is different than we thought.

## Course Overview

[[Course The Slow]] 

This course is about other worldly redemption, virtual love and sacrifice, and real sanctification.

Narrative vs. literal reading of the course.


This course introduces undergraduate students to philosophy by using **narrative thought experiments**. We use David Chalmers' *Reality+: Virtual Worlds and the Problems of Philosophy* as our primary text, supplemented with classical philosophical works and contemporary readings.

In this book, Chalmers asks the reader whether or not the simulation hypothesis, the view that we could be living inside a simulation, is a viable one. He covers reasons for and against this hypothesis that span the philosophical discipline. 

The literal point of the book, is an exploration of the philosophical discipline. It includes major questions, attempts at solving these problems. Uniquely, the way that Chalmers’ does this, is by using technology to understand developments in philosophy, and uses philosophy to understand developments in technology. At the core of this investigation, is the simulation hypothesis.

**Core Thesis**: Philosophy is a practice of rigorous thinking designed to cultivate intellectual virtue—the willingness to suspend assumptions, ask fundamental questions, and wrestle with ideas both abstractly and subjectively. Through game-based thought experiments, and other narrative media, we will learn that ideas have real consequences for how we understand knowledge, reality, mind, and value.

**Laboratory Component: **As such, this course contains a laboratory component. The laboratory component of the course involves demonstration and application of a theory in a defined physical/virtual setting (e.g., video game/simulation). Students explore and experiment under the guidance of the instructor, and the class size is limited by division into course sections. The laboratory component of the course focuses on the development and creation of virtual work in a dynamic virtual environment.

**Pedagogical Approach**: Rather than passively reading about philosophy, you will *do* philosophy by:

1. Encountering philosophical thought experiments embedded in narrative environments
2. Designing your own game-based thought experiments based on course readings
3. Creating and designing AI philosopher personas that ask Socratic questions
4. Collaborating with peers to explore philosophical problems through interactive game-play
5. Reflecting on how your intuitions change through philosophical reasoning

This approach treats philosophy as **experiential learning**—you don't just understand the trolley problem abstractly; you participate in it. You don't just read about the experience machine; you explore it as an experience inside virtual environments.

## Course Learning Objectives

By the end of this course, you will be able to:

6. **Identify and articulate philosophical problems** in classical and contemporary texts
7. **Recognize thought experiments** as tools for philosophical reasoning and evaluate their effectiveness
8. **Apply philosophical concepts** (skepticism, realism, dualism, utilitarianism, etc.) to novel scenarios
9. **Design and create Socratic dialogue** that challenge your assumptions
10. **Design thought experiments** using a variety of media including dialogue that illuminate philosophical problems
11. **Reflect critically on your own intuitions** and how reasoning changes them
12. **Collaborate effectively** in philosophical discussions and creative narrative design
13. **Articulate philosophical positions** clearly in writing and discussion

## Assignment Details

### 1. Weeklies (120 points total)

**Due**: Every other week (6 assignments throughout semester)

**Format**: Individual written submissions (500-750 words)

**Task**: Design a game event based on a thought experiment from your readings

**Each weekly includes:**

- Identification of a thought experiment and its source
- What the philosopher intended to prove
- Your initial intuition on the philosophical question
- Description of the game event you designed (what would happen in the game?)
- Your intuition after engaging with the thought experiment
- Reflection: Was the thought experiment convincing? Did it confirm or change your intuition? Why?

**Grading Rubric (20 points per weekly):**

- Thought experiment identification & analysis (5 pts)
- Game design clarity & engagement (5 pts)
- Philosophical reasoning (5 pts)
- Reflection & intuition tracking (5 pts)

---

### 2. Game Sessions (45 points total)

**Frequency**: 3 sessions throughout semester (assigned dates on calendar)

**Format**: Group participation (3 students per group) + individual reflection

**Duration**: 45-60 min gameplay + written reflection

**What happens:**

- Your group plays through a philosopher-guided game experience in the Experience Machine
- The experience tests your intuitions on a philosophical problem
- You reflect collectively on your experience

**Deliverable**: Short reflection (400-600 words) including:

- Topic of the philosophical problem
- Which philosopher/persona guided the experience
- Your initial intuition (before)
- The game event/scenario
- Your intuition after
- Why did (or didn't) the experience change your thinking?

**Grading Rubric (15 points each):**

- Engagement & reflection clarity (15 pts)
- Philosophical insight (15 pts)
- Connection between game experience & intuition change (15 pts)

---

### 3. Philosopher Personas (75 points total)

**Frequency**: 3 persona creations (once per game session cycle)

**Format**: Group project (3 students)

**Duration**: 2-week development cycle for each persona

**What you do:**

14. Draw a philosopher's name from a hat (not a philosopher you're reading weekly)
15. Spend 2 weeks researching that philosopher's position on a specific philosophical problem
16. Create a **Claude AI persona** that embodies that philosopher
17. Write the system prompt that instructs Claude to ask Socratic questions (not give answers) in the style of that philosopher
18. **Test your persona in the provided Git template**: Have peers interact with it and provide feedback
19. Refine based on testing
20. Submit: System prompt + documentation + testing feedback

**Example**: You draw "Immanuel Kant." Your task is to create a Kant persona that, when asked "Is morality objective?", responds with Socratic questions like:

- "Tell me, what do you mean by 'objective'? Does it require universal agreement?"
- "Hmm, but couldn't something be universally obligatory without depending on our desires?"
- "Have you considered the distinction between hypothetical and categorical imperatives?"

**Grading Rubric (25 points each):**

- Philosophical accuracy of persona (8 pts)
- Quality of Socratic questioning (8 pts)
- Clarity of system prompt documentation (5 pts)
- Evidence of testing & iteration (4 pts)

---

### 4. Final Project (80 points)

**Due**: Last day of class (12/11/2026)

**Format**: Individual written project (~2500-3000 words)

**Structure**: Three-philosopher exploration

**Your task:**

21. **Choose ONE philosophical topic** (e.g., "Is consciousness physical?" or "Can knowledge be objective?" or "Do moral truths exist?")
22. **Select THREE philosophers** you've encountered in the course
23. **For each philosopher**, structure your analysis as:
	- **Philosopher's Position**: What does this philosopher argue about your topic?
	- **Your Initial Intuition** (before reading): What did you think before encountering this view?
	- **Game Experience**: Describe a game scenario (from class or one you design) that explored this philosopher's position
	- **Your Intuition After**: How did engaging with the game-based thought experiment affect your thinking?
	- **Why?**: Explain what about the game experience changed (or confirmed) your intuition
24. **Synthesis Section** (final 500-750 words):
	- Summary of each philosopher's position
	- Which philosopher's view do you find most convincing? Why?
	- Which did you initially resist? Why?
	- Your final position on the topic and how you arrived at it
	- Reflection on the role of thought experiments in your philosophical development

**Grading Rubric (80 points):**

- Philosophical accuracy & depth (20 pts)
- Quality of game-experience reflection (20 pts)
- Clarity of intuition tracking across philosophers (20 pts)
- Synthesis & original thinking (15 pts)
- Writing quality & organization (5 pts)

## Philosophy as Moral Practice

Throughout this course, we approach philosophy as more than intellectual exercise. Philosophy is a **moral practice**—a discipline that cultivates virtue by:

- **Intellectual humility**: Recognizing what we don't know
- **Rigor**: Following arguments wherever they lead
- **Openness**: Genuinely considering views that challenge our own
- **Wonder**: Maintaining awe at life's deep questions
- **Community**: Building the "union of love appropriate to friendship" through shared inquiry

When we engage with thought experiments, we're not just testing logical consistency. We're training ourselves to think deeply, to imagine alternatives, and to recognize that ideas have real consequences for how we live.

This is what makes philosophy transformative.

## Final Thought

Philosophy begins with wonder. It's the practice of standing still, suspending what we think we know, and asking: "But what if I'm wrong? What does this really mean? Why does it matter?"

In this course, we do that together—through reading, discussion, game-based thought experiments, and dialogue with philosophers past and present, who ask better questions than we ask ourselves.

Welcome to philosophy. Let's think deeply about what matters.

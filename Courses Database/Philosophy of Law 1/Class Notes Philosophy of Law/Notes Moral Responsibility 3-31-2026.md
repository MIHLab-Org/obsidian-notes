# Philosophy of Law: Criminal


• Marshall, S. E., and R. A. Duff, 1998, “Criminalization and Sharing Wrongs”, *Canadian Journal of Law & Jurisprudence*, 11: 7–22.

“Blackstone had a point in identifying crimes as public wrongs and torts as private wrongs.” (Marshall and Duff, 1998, p. 1)

“Many claim that if φ-ing is a crime, then φ-ing is legally wrongful. It is something that, in the eyes of the law, ought not to be done (Hart 1994, 27; Gardner 2007, 239; Tadros 2016, 91).” (Edwards, 2021, p. 1) (pdf)

“The criminal law should deal with those kinds of wrong which are matters** of public concern**, and which therefore require collective responses from the whole community; wrongs which are, by contrast, properly the concern only of the private or individual victim should be dealt with under the civil law.” (Marshall and Duff, 1998, p. 1)

Imagine **D** is about to φ. If φ-ing is a crime, reasonable force may permissibly be used to prevent φ-ing. Police officers and private persons alike have powers to arrest, and reasonable force may permissibly be used to make arrests effective.

“Our main concern in this paper is, therefore,** the concept of crime,** and we do not directly engage in the debate about the distinction between crime and tort.” (Marshall and Duff, 1998, p. 2)

Criminal law should not infringe on life, liberty, property, or health. It should not directly endanger any of these.

The Law defines what infringement means in these cases.

“The criminal law** should proscribe intrusions into important individual ‘Rechtsgüter’ such as life, liberty, property and health.**” (Marshall and Duff, 1998, p. 2)

“Proscribe conduct which directly endangers.” (Marshall and Duff, 1998, p. 2)

---

When it comes to criminal law, the state has both** positive and negative duties and obligations: **not to infringe, but also to promote collective and public goods and rights. To define infringement.

> [!note]+ [w]hen I am talking *directly* of the chance of incurring the evil, or (changing the expression) of the liability or obnoxiousness to the evil, I employ the term *duty*, or the term *obligation…*. (Austin 1832 [1995: 18]; see also Bentham *Of Laws in General*; Hobbes 1651 [1968])


---

If their content does not account for the stringency of obligations, what does? **An historically important, though now largely defunct, theory explained it in terms of penalty. According to this view, to have a legal obligation is to be subject to a sovereign command to do or forbear, where a command requires an expression of will together with an attached risk, however small, of suffering an evil for non-compliance.**

---

The sovereign commandment defines what constitutes infringement by a potential penalty. 

As the English jurist John Austin puts it,

> [w]hen I am talking *directly* of the chance of incurring the evil, or (changing the expression) of the liability or obnoxiousness to the evil, I employ the term *duty*, or the term *obligation…*. (Austin 1832 [1995: 18]; see also Bentham *Of Laws in General*; Hobbes 1651 [1968])

**As such, to have a duty or obligation is to be subject to sanction.**

**Others conceived an indirect connection between duty and sanction,** holding that what is normally counted as the content of a legal duty is in reality only part of a triggering condition for the mandatory norm which commands or authorizes officials to impose a sanction. According to this view,

> a norm: “You shall not murder” is superfluous, if a norm is valid: “He who murders ought to be punished”. (Kelsen 1960 [1967: 55])

Thus,

> [l]egal obligation is not, or not immediately, the behavior that ought to be. Only the coercive act, functioning as a sanction, ought to be. (Kelsen 1960 [1967: 119])

**None of these versions of the sanction theory survived H.L.A. Hart’s criticisms** (Hart 1961 [1994: 27–42]; cf. Hacker 1973; but see Schauer 2015 for a recent reformulation of this approach).** First, they misleadingly represent a range of disparate legal consequences**—including compensation and even invalidation—as if they all function as penalties. 

- **Second, they render unintelligible many familiar references to duties in the absence of sanctions,** for example, the duty of the highest courts to apply the law. 
- **Third, they offer an inadequate explanation of non-optionality.** “You have an obligation not to murder” cannot merely mean “If you murder you will be punished”,** for the law is not indifferent between people, on the one hand, murdering and being jailed, and on the other hand not murdering at all.** “The right to disobey the law is not obtainable by the payment of a penalty or a licence fee” (*Francome v. Mirror Group Newspapers Ltd.* [1984] 2 All ER 408 at 412). 

**The normal function of sanctions in the law is to reinforce duties, not to constitute them.** It is true that *one* reason people are interested in knowing their legal duties is to avoid sanctions, but this is not the only reason nor is it, contrary to what Oliver Wendell Holmes supposed, a theoretically primary one (Holmes 1897). Subjects also want to be *guided by* their duties—whether in order to fulfil them or deliberately to infringe them—and officials invoke them as *reasons for*, and not merely consequences of, their decisions.

Sensitivity to such matters led Hart to defend a *rule-based* theory. He says that while sanctions might mark circumstances in which people are *obliged* to conform, they have *an obligation* only when subject to a practised social rule requiring an act or omission.

“What the individual victim has suffered, and to (mis)portray crimes simply as acts of bad citizenship.” (Marshall and Duff, 1998, p. 2)

“That such wrongs injure important Rechtsgüter: the state has a duty to use the criminal law to promote respect for such significant individual rights.” (Marshall and Duff, 1998, p. 2)

“This perspective also finds room for at least some ‘public’ or ‘collective’ goods that the criminal law should protect.” (Marshall and Duff, 1998, p. 2)

“By proscribing and punishing attacks on individual Rechtsgüter, it serves at the same time to assure all citizens that such wrongs against them will not go unpunished, and thus to foster collective security.” (Marshall and Duff, 1998, p. 2)

“Now one objection to this perspective on the criminal law is that it does not do justice to the idea of genuinely public or collective goods, which cannot be reduced to aggregates of individual goods: goods belonging to a ‘we’ which cannot be adequately understood as a collection of discrete and individual ‘I’s; goods which matter to us not as aspects of our merely individual well-being, but as members of a community; goods which are goods of that community.” (Marshall and Duff, 1998, p. 2)

“The first claim is that even if some ‘public’ goods could be analysed as aggregates of individual goods, others resist such reductive analysis: consider, for just one instance, the idea of a thriving culture as a ‘public’ or common good.” (Marshall and Duff, 1998, p. 2)

A number of philosophers are concerned with normative theories of rational choice. Eleonore Stump anticipates an approach that deviates from standard ones in epistemology. Professor Stump bases her approach on narrative. She argues that analytic philosophy can become too narrow and myopic, excluding “complex, nuanced thought, behavior, and [or necessary for understanding] relations of persons” (Stump 2010, 23). Here I draw from popular narrative and attempt to make light of this philosophically rich narrative account in light of a rational choice theory of choice. 

One specific problem in rational choice theory, is the fact that we are often wrong about our priorities. We see this problematic outcome when exercising one particular advice plan of rational choice theory, that we should choose whichever action maximizes value. However, we are often mistaken about our priorities. Rational choice theory says that we should follow a plan that maximizes the value of the outcome. As the problem goes, we are often mistaken about what values to maximize. In the context of special personal relationships, this common error ought to be at the heart of the literature on suffering. This makes it especially difficult when trying to decide what to do [[[Notion/Moral Imagination and Hope Laboratory/Notero Advanced/Planning for Mistakes]], 1]. We might follow some plan of action, only to find out that the promised outcome is not what we desired after all. Such mistakes in judgment then, stem from failures to recognize our own priorities. In other words, they stem from errors in judgment about ourselves. 

There are many versions of this concern. For instance, lets imagine that on the basis of a coinflip, we will either be shown a genuine or a counterfeit Rothko. If we are shown the counterfeit but not the genuine version, we would be uncertain about its authenticity and assign equal credence to either possibility. Therefore, we might mistakenly think that upon looking at a counterfeit Rothko, that we desire to bid on the counterfeit Rothko rather than a genuine one. 

According to some, whether or not these mistakes are avoidable is an open question as it is plausible that a key kind of evidence necessary to make correct judgments about ourselves, is not a part of the set of evidence that we have. The kind of common mistake that we make, which is important for Stump’s thesis, is that an opaque decision problem with respect to decision theory concerns special personal relationships.

We can imagine some agent that wants to believe some proposition *p* about themselves, for instance that they are in love, but for all *p*, -*p* is not a part of the agent’s evidence. ### Introduction

Awe is an epistemic emotion that we experience when we perceive or conceptualize vastness and need to cognitively accommodate our perceptions. One evening, yourself and some friends see an unfamiliar dirt road leading into what you know to be, for you, an unexplored area of the Southern California high desert. It’s late, around 9pm, and this area is dark. You press on. The headlights from the car that you are driving give you an unwarranted sense of security amidst the pitch darkness of the desert around you. The road narrows somewhat and you notice a stark rise in elevation. There are several switchbacks as you continue to gain altitude. Finally the road ends at a small plateau which you ascend and find yourself on level ground. You get out of the car and turn off the car’s lights looking up towards the clear night sky, at which point you have an unobstructed and unpolluted view of the night sky above. There’s nothing to discover, or understand about what lies before you.

Similar to awe, wonder is also an epistemic emotion that we experience when we glimpse at something that is similar but still unknown to us. You are walking along a walking trail near your home when you see for the first time, an uncommon creature such as a snake of unusual hue and coloration. You stand in the same spot for several minutes while you try to think of all you have seen that is closely related to the object in front of you, and all that you might have heard. 

Both emotions are important for the mental states related to knowledge. These include curiosity, doubt, surprise etc. It has been argued that either science or technology, can cultivate or threaten these experiences [[Wonderstruck How Wonder and Awe Shape the Way We Think]]; [[Gabriel Marcel]]. There is a sense in which both of these emotions are important for special relationships. Parent, child, spouse, close friend, etc. It is also plausible to argue that these emotional states are synonymous with the state of being that we typically characterize as “in love”. But before we provide a defense of this description of love, we should look at other possibilities. 

Another way to understand this, is that the insights of science or meaningful lyrics as a cognitive technology, can help us to discover new sources of awe and wonder [[Wonderstruck How Wonder and Awe Shape the Way We Think]]. De Cruz argues that cognitive technologies related to awe and wonder are a cultural means “to control our emotions” (Wonder, 14). In this way, cognitive technologies can either help or hinder our ability to make important decisions. For instance, scientists regularly use models to test their intuitions. Political scientists might build a political model of a particular election in order to test their intuition about the viability of running one candidate over another. A military strategist might create a model of a military campaign to test their battle strategy. 

Following Stump 2010, suggests that biblical narratives are cognitive technologies that enable deeper relationships with other people. This in turn allows us to make informed opaque decisions, decisions in situations where an agent is uncertain about their own credences. I attempt to test this claim by looking at an example of music lyrics by treating them as a kind of decision technology.

Some have argued that when we lose our sense of wonder, our sense of what defines us is lost. According to Gabriel Marcel, [[Gabriel Marcel]]; [[Gabriel (-Honoré) Marcel]]; [[Marcel, 1963]]; [[Marcel]]; as we look at another individual who would naturally represent a deep source of awe and wonder for us, we instead see a set of functions and sources of productivity. When our sense of wonder is lost, our sense of what defines us as humans is also lost.

Emotions are important for survival. They allow for adaptive responses to threats and opportunities in our environment, including social ones (Ekman 1992). Awe and wonder are *self-transcendent emotions, *they move our attention away from ourselves facilitating more adaptive group participation. This happens when these technologies can cause inappropriate emotional states. In other words, technology that may be the source of our failure to recognize the value in things that should be valuable. As humans, it is likely that we find special relationships essential for flourishing. Therefore, failing to recognize the value in special relationships is a failure to appreciate what is essential for human flourishing. Therefore science and technology undermines our sense of what defines us as human beings, such as we thrive in environments of mutual particular care.  

Helen De Cruz, [[Helen De Cruz (St. Louis University), Wonderstruck]]  argues that this story about science is incomplete. The technologies in question either help us to make informed opaque decisions. One way they do this, is that the insights of science enable us to discover new sources of wonder. We are imperfectly reliable “at identifying our own credences, utilities, and available acts”. According to Maximize Expected Utility, or Maximize Utility, we might then think that some songs as a technology can help or hurt our abilities to make informed decisions. Cognitive technologies serve human needs such as navigating our environment [[Wonderstruck How Wonder and Awe Shape the Way We Think]], 99. Therefore it is possible that cognitive technologies can serve human needs by helping us to support our credences.  By treating lyrics instrumentally, commercial music production for instance, songs may help us to find or lose our sense of awe and wonder with regard to special relationships, they may help or hinder our ability to recognize the inherent value in this relationships by revealing to ourselves whether we are vulnerable creatures or not. For instance, a cognitive technology like the popular song “**By yourself”, **might remove the pain behind a recent breakup. The importance of this is being in control of our thoughts and emotions, “controlling our emotions before they control us” [[On romantic love]], 199. 

The problem here though, is that while a technology like the song “**By Yourself”** can help us to control our emotions, they may do so by revealing to us that we are not in fact relationally vulnerable. As such, we still need a way to determine whether we are relationally vulnerable or relationally invulnerable creatures. So songs may be the kinds of technologies that allow us to “control our emotions” when we are uncertain about our credences and expected values.

The first represents an emergent (like emergentist about mind) claim about the role of the science and technology in our loss of wonder, namely that the purpose of science and technology is to dispel our sense of awe and wonder (giving us control over our emotions) and what Helen De Cruz is saying, is that this view of science conflates the object, discovery, with a potentially indirect outcome. 

Here, e.g., we might think that country music is a kind of technology that induces awe and wonder at special relationships, or mitigates awe and wonder on the prospect of a painful breakup, either of which may be adaptive.  We can operationalize this outcome and ask whether there is a direct, or merely indirect relationship between science and apathy (a form of suffering). It warrants exploring to determine what each proposes of the relationship between scientific discovery, or awe and wonder technology and one’s loss of meaning.

Decision theory specifies which acts are rational given an agent’s prior credences and utilities. However, sometimes an agent’s prior credences are uncertain.

> “An opaque decision problem represents the situation of an agent who must decide what to do while being (potentially) uncertain about their own credences”.

What that agent needs then is an opaque decision theory for when an agent has imperfect self-knowledge (uncertain about credences, utilities, and available acts). Normative theory of rational choice says that in deciding what to do we could either maximize expected value or maximize actual value. Maximize actual Value is often hard advice to follow. We are often uncertain about which acts available to us, will have the best outcome. How an agent acts depends on what plan they are trying to follow. 

However, sometimes, they instead perform an act which their plan recommends at another state. Decision theorists often cite Bayesian statistics to model decision theories when their is a degree of uncertainty.  However, we do not always know whether our doxastic states are rational or not, do not always have sufficient evidence. Therefore, orthodox Bayesians have a difficulty modelling a certain kind of uncertainty, when our credences are unknown (akrasia).

In the first, the agent made a decision that they regretted. In the second, the agent made a similar decision as the first but did not regret it. Suppose that you are an agent who understands what some advice plan recommends in any possible circumstance and that you fully intend to follow the advice plan that will maximize you expected value. However, you do not know what your circumstances are and therefore you do not know what your "maximized expected value" will look like. This represents an opaque decision problem $\Delta$ = $\langle \text{S}^{\hat{\Delta}}, O^{\hat{\Delta}}, A^{\hat{\Delta}}, P^{\hat{\Delta}}, \upsilon^{\hat{\Delta}}\rangle$ which comprises:

A set of worlds, $\Omega$.
A set of states, $S$.
A set of outcomes, $O$.
A set of acts, A $\subseteq$ $S$ $\rightarrow$ $O$.
A state-dependent probability function, P: $S$ $\rightarrow$ $(P(S)$ $\rightarrow$ [0,1]).
A value function, $\upsilon$: $O \rightarrow \R$

For opaque decision problem $\hat{\Delta}$: $\Pi = S \rightarrow A$ is the set of plans determined by $\hat\Delta$ where a plan is a function from states s, s',s'',s''',. . . $\in S$, and each s $\in S$, $\langle S, O, A, P-{s}, \upsilon \rangle$ is a standard Savage style decision problem that represents the agent's perspective at $s$ on the world.  Where a plan, $\pi, \pi ', \pi '', . . . \in \Pi$ are functions from states to acts. Given $\pi(s) = a$, we say that $a$ is the act recommended by $\pi$ in $s$.

Maximize expected value, $\Pi_{|MEV}$ is the set of plans to maximize expected value in $\hat{\Delta}$. $ev_{s}(a)$ is the expected value of $a$ at $s$ for any $s \in S$ and $s \in A$.

The expected value of an act form the perspective of $s$ is the sum of the values of the outcomes produced by the act at each state where each is weighted according to the probability assigned to that state from the perspective of $s$.

$$
\begin{equation}
ev_{s}(a) = \sum^{}_{s'\in S} \upsilon(a(s'))\bullet P_{s}(s')
\end{equation}
$$

Given $\Pi_{|MEV}$, then $\pi \in \Pi_{|MEV}$ iff for all $s \in S$ and $a \in A$:

$$
\begin{equation}
ev_{s}(\pi (s)) \geq ev_{s}(a)
\end{equation}
$$

## Setting Up the Problem

The bet pays at 2:3 odds: a $1 stake returns $1.67 total, so a **net gain of $0.67** on a win, and a **net loss of $1** on a loss. This implied probability of winning is:

$$
p_{\text{win}} = \frac{1}{1.67} = \frac{3}{5} = 0.6
$$

Let $p$ be the probability of "Marriage" being the higher-value choice. The expected values are:

$$
EV(\text{Marriage}) = 0.67p - 1(1-p) = \tfrac{5}{3}p - 1
$$

$$
EV(\text{Career}) = -1\cdot p + 0.67(1-p) = \tfrac{2}{3} - \tfrac{5}{3}p
$$

$$
EV(\text{Decline}) = 0
$$

## The Self-Referential Trap

The key feature is that **your own choice determines the outcome** — you cannot know in advance which act truly maximizes value, so you have no genuine informational edge. Effectively $p = \tfrac{1}{2}$ (you are symmetric between the two options). Then:

$$
EV(\text{Marriage}) = \tfrac{5}{3}\cdot\tfrac{1}{2} - 1 = \tfrac{5}{6} - 1 = -\tfrac{1}{6}
$$

$$
EV(\text{Career}) = \tfrac{2}{3} - \tfrac{5}{3}\cdot\tfrac{1}{2} = \tfrac{2}{3} - \tfrac{5}{6} = -\tfrac{1}{6}
$$

Both wagering acts yield the same **negative** expected value, so the bet loses money regardless of which side you take.

$$
\boxed{EV = -\tfrac{1}{6} \approx -\$0.17}
$$

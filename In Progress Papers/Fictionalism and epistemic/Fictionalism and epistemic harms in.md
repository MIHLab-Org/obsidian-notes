[[Aesthetics of Evil: Medieval Bestiaries and Epistemic Injustice (Presentation)]]

## Abstract

In the article,[[Aesthetics of Evil in Middle Ages]], Ricardo Pinero Moral highlights the importance of representing beasts as admonitions to avoid evil in medieval bestiaries. He argues that in medieval bestiaries, there are two classes of evil represented by artistic renderings of animals. There are those answers that represent harms synonymous with deception and those harms that are synonymous with the fear of death, and death itself. Among the first class of beasts, those which are notable for their abilities to deceive, there are three kinds of deception that cause epistemic harms to the targets: these are noted by the camouflages of the chameleon, accurate imitations of the manticore, and the “seductive skills of sirens”.

- **Deceivers**: Camouflage, imitation, seduction
	- Chameleon: camouflage
	- Manticore: accurate imitations
	- Sirens: seductive skills
- **Fear and Death Generators**

Moral contrasts the kinds of epistemic harms the medievalists worried about from contemporary epistemic harms and risks. These include misinformation, false and inauthentic narratives, and bullsh*t. The medieval bestiary is an attempt to admonish a pre-literate population against these specific harms. This article attempts to develop an account of inauthentic narrative as a moral harm that was recognized by church leaders in the middle ages.

# What is art if not beauty?

The non-skeptic in our tradition asks “what is art if not beauty? What this question would have us conclude then, is that genuine art reflects on the concept of beauty, and therefore beauty is an essential trait or function of art. But many aestheticians in our period reject the notion of beauty as the hallmark of genuine and worthy art; and they also turn their backs on the tradition of essentialist theories, these are theories that propose specific traits or functions as critical to works of art. Though they ask the question “What is art?”” (“Aesthetics: A Comprehensive Anthology”, 2020, p. 433), it is an open discussion about whether they reject any reductionism between art and beauty, debating whether art concepts reduce to ones about beauty.

Ricardo Piñero Moral ([2021a](http://localhost:6369/papers/paper-1-aes-evi/#ref-pine21b)) deviates somewhat from the norm in his analysis of medieval bestiaries. What we call the bestiary, is part of a tradition that stems back to individuals like Ctesias and Aristotle who compiled reports they received about the inhabitants of lands that were far away. There are many interesting topics of discussion surrounding the creation of bestiaries and their early taxonomic uses.  These include the depictions of humans and their interaction with exotic and fantastic animals as the creators of a given bestiary would have imagined, the gold leaf that is sometimes used on the edges of their pages, and the sometimes comical stories their pictures depict. Rather than focusing on their visual imagery however, Moral seeks to develop an account of the meaning which the medieval artists and authors sought to uncover in his analysis of their contents. He focuses on the harms represented as beasts depicted in bestiaries. More importantly he focuses on the warnings of evil in the medieval mind. 

As such, Moral’s paper is a valid way to begin discourse on the topic of this collection. Notable among Moral’s aims in his discussion of harm is deception, imitation, illusion, and seduction. Looking at harms grounded in a kind of deception, what is important here is that the three beasts associated with deception are also associated with pleasure. The relationship is obvious. It is a situation where we have the promise of pleasure, namely seduction, to the end of the flourishing of the victim, the resulting harm. The two specific animals he considers are the manticore which commits the sin of imitation while the siren plays the seductress.

### The Physiologus and its Contemporaries

Bestiaries notably are medieval texts that stem from an earlier tradition in classical Greek literature, especially the animal taxonomies created by Aristotle and Ctesias. Sometime around 343 BCE, Aristotle and a student of his, Theophrastus, would found a school called the *Lyceum*. They would then go on to develop what are now known to be the biological sciences with Aristotle focusing treatises on the systematic reflections on animal anatomy, physiology, and their development with Theophrastus doing the same for plants. 

In total, Aristotle is generally credited with two classifications of work on the biological sciences. Among the first class, these include *History of Animals; Parts of Animals, *and *Generation of Animals. *Among the second class are his shorter and more specialized works; *On animal motion, On animal locomotion, On respiration, On life and death, On youth and old age, On length and shortness of life, On sleeping and waking, On the senses and their objects. *

Of particular importance for the medieval ages, is Book II of Aristotle’s *History of the Animals. *However, these did not become the bestiaries we know of today. Rather, it was that until the production of the account known as the *Physiologus, that *the creatures we find in the bestiaries of the medieval period where merely scientific treatises, although incomplete in some ways. It was believed that these animals were thought to exist by their authors, or perhaps as in Aristotle’s case, disputed the written record. The manticore is one such creature, of whom Aristotle disputes the written record. 

 On some accounts, the manticore is a union between a hyena and Ethiopian lioness. It imitates the voices of men and possesses a fixed and immoveable gaze. In addition, it does not possess gums in either of its jaws, and rather than having multiple teeth, it has one large one that curves around its jaw bone.

We might think that medieval art has no place in a discussion about epistemic injustice, but the depiction of the fictional beast called the manticore, as a personification of deception lends credibility to the claim that medieval art is an early representation of the dangers of epistemic harms, an early representation of the extent to which early authorities recognized epistemic harms. According to the modern function of art summed up by Gilmore ([2020](http://localhost:6369/papers/paper-1-aes-evi/#ref-gilm20)), art’s function is oriented to ends such as pleasure, entertainment, and absorption [132]. Conversely, the medieval function of art is directed at more orthodox ends intending to motivate positive emotion states, or desirable behaviors and behavioral responses.

According to some hermeneutic views centering on epistemic injustice, because of a misappropriated power imbalance, a person holds false beliefs about themselves. These beliefs stem from unjust environments where the individual lacks access to resources that would lead to accurate self-understanding and moral beliefs. Such beliefs are rooted in irrational actions performed by that individual. One such concept of hermeneutic justice has been popularized by Miranda Fricker ([2007](http://localhost:6369/papers/paper-1-aes-evi/#ref-fric07)) though it was also developed by earlier feminists. As an artistic object, the Manticore is a fictionalized beast that was born in Persian art and literature sometime around its cultural zenith. It at once symbolizes evil, death and hell, but in medieval religious art, it had come to symbolize the power of Christ over death and hell.

# **Fairy Tales and Fiction**

What are the characteristics that distinguish fiction from non-fiction (Gregory Currie, 1990). This question is a close investigation of the entities that are in fiction and make them possible. For instance, the psychological and linguistic resources that producers and consumers of fiction must have access to.

First, consider that fiction uses language. But there is no universal linguistic feature shared by all fictional works and there is no available proof/s confirming what is a fiction. We usually think that reading a work is an act of reading words and sentences that make it up. However, consider a mime? Or silent films? These are works that do not use language in the traditional sense, yet we consider them fictional. Therefore, what makes something a fiction cannot necessarily be a linguistic feature. What makes something a fiction is neither a necessary nor sufficient condition for being fictional. Finally then, something can be a fiction because of its verbal structure, but what makes it a fiction does not lie in the text itself.

On this account, fictional properties, like non-fictional ones, have the semantic properties of truth value and reference. What this means is that a fictional statement can be true or false. For instance, consider the statement that one might here in reference to the open-world space sim Star Citizen: “Killing an NPC in monitored space will earn you a crime stat”. According to Currie, this statement is true. What makes something true or false in a fiction, is its relation to the outside world and so what is important then, is what this relationship is. In the context of playing the game Star Citizen, a more senior player may say to a less seasoned player “be careful that you do not fire on any non-aggressive non-player characters, doing so can earn you a crime stat”.

Because fiction is a relational property, they are community based and “depend on the prevailing attitudes of the community, or of a subgroup of the community rather than the ideas of a single individual” ([Currie 1990, 10](http://localhost:6369/papers/paper-1-aes-evi/#ref-curr90)). The above statements would be meaningless in any other context than in Star Citizen, yet Star Citizen grounds their semantic content. While they would be meaningless in another context, in the context of Star Citizen, they would either be true or false.

> For I take it that an act of joint authorship is exactly that: an act engaged in by more than one person rather than several distinct acts undertaken individually and patched together. ([Currie 1990, 11](http://localhost:6369/papers/paper-1-aes-evi/#ref-curr90))

The process of creating a fiction begins with the intention. It is not a singular intention however, but is a social enterprise. So maybe the reason that we do not call fake news, fiction, is because of the purposes and intent of fake news. It is intended to deceive rather than express true propositions. But if this is true, then why do we not consider that fiction is intended to deceive? Why does fiction have semantic properties such as truth whereas fake news does not? Discovering the characteristics that distinguish fiction from non-fiction are a close investigation of the entities that are in fiction and make them possible. For instance, the psychological and linguistic resources that producers and consumers of fiction must have access to.

Owing to the fantastical nature of the medieval accounts found inside bestiaries, it is highly probable that they are either misguided or motivated by some purposes other than truth. In the 57th question of the 1st of the second part of the Summa Theologica, Thomas Aquinas defends the view of art as a form of right reason. Accordingly then, a view of art then is truth as expressed through right reason. There is a distinction here between the right reason for making objects, and the right reason for doing good actions. We should first focus on the point important to both of these activities, the right use of one’s cognitive faculties. The difference lies in the produced object, whether an action or thing.

If we are to ask ourselves what justifies our emotional responses to some given experience, on the one hand we might consider that the validity of the experience rests in something like the authenticity of the experience. Assuming that artistic experiences are driven by the same norms as other experiences, the image of the Manticore would generate fear in those who were lucky enough to witness an artistic representation of one, whether literary or visual. However, artistic experiences are not always governed by the same norms as other kinds of experiences. Some additional norms that govern our experiences with fiction include pleasure, entertainment, and absorption ([Gilmore 2020, 132](http://localhost:6369/papers/paper-1-aes-evi/#ref-gilm20)). However, this would mark an important divergence from the functions of art accepted by the medievalist.

Currie concludes that fictionality is a relational property, it is something possessed in virtue of the text’s relationship to other things. Therefore while it is possible that fictionality is a linguistic matter, what makes it a fiction lies in some other property. Fictional properties, like non-fictional ones, have the semantic properties of truth value and reference. What this means is that a fictional statement, like other kinds of propositions can be true and false. For instance, consider the statement “Killing an NPC in monitored space will earn you a crime stat”. According to Currie, this statement is true. What makes something true or false in a fiction, is its relation to the outside world and what this relationship is.

The difference lies in the produced object, whether an action or thing. But here, we should pause momentarily or we risk conflating two senses of right action. In the ancient context, right action would more probably be used to reference actions that align with a dominant organizational structure, for instance the Catholic church. In our own modern context, we are less apt to recognize a singular dominant moral authority. This presents a problem since we would want to say something more akin to ‘beneficial’, or ‘productive’ action where ‘benefit’ and ‘product’ are more expressive of individual tastes and desires, or are contextual.

Considering a product that is produced, the desired outcome of the activity of art, it is not clear what it is exactly, that we collectively care about in the case of art then. When it comes to what is produced by artistic endeavor, it is not clear whether a normative account should include a material commodity, or a desired outcome apart from a material commodity. When we think about what a good artistic endeavor entails, in the modern conception, there is typically some monetary component. For instance, an admirable artistic endeavor at the center of an advertising campaign would ideally result in increased revenue for the advertising clients and increased business for the advertiser.

Pinero evokes a Thomistic functionalist account of art. Accordingly, the medieval conception of an admirable artistic endeavor consists of outcomes which invoke some kind of behavior and as such, the function of the medieval artistic endeavors include outcomes connected to admirable behavior. Whether these include dispositions or actions, ultimately there is a desire for an intended outcome as it correlates specifically with a human actor.

Consider that fiction uses language. But there is no universal linguistic feature shared by all fictional works and there is no available proof/s confirming what is a fiction. We usually think that reading a work is an act of reading words and sentences that make it up. However, consider a mime, or silent films. These are works that do not use language in the traditional sense, yet we consider them fictional. Therefore, what makes something a fiction cannot necessarily be a linguistic feature. What makes something a fiction is neither a necessary nor sufficient condition for being fictional. Finally then, something can be a fiction because of its verbal structure, but what makes it a fiction does not lie in the text itself.

Harrison ([2011](http://localhost:6369/papers/paper-1-aes-evi/#ref-harr11)) argues for instance, that Elizabeth Gaskell’s novel Mary Barton: A Tale of Manchester Life was in part responsible for the UK’s expanded social safety net. Gaskell represented the suffering and distress experienced by the working poor in a way that allowed her upper-class Victorian audience to empathize with the lower social classes ([Harrison 2011, 258](http://localhost:6369/papers/paper-1-aes-evi/#ref-harr11)). We might think that a good artist produces items of great monetary value. But using the medieval depiction as expressed above, it seems that a good artist instead motivates its audience to behave in a way that produces good outcomes, or leads to flourishing or some other expression of well-being. So in focusing on medieval depictions of the manticore, we can now see art as it is used to guard against evil. This evil however is a warning against deception. I argue that it is this distinctive kind of epistemic harm/injustice that consists of undermining our ability to convey knowledge to others and make sense of our own experiences ([Fricker 2007](http://localhost:6369/papers/paper-1-aes-evi/#ref-fric07)).

# **On Greek Tragedy**

As I’ve mentioned before, the classical world often treats narrative texts differently than the modern world. However, Martha Nussbaum in her book ‘The Fragility of Goodness’ has defended a novel interpretation of what we might think to be modern aesthetic practice found in classical Greek literature. She first notes three important sources of narrative conflict in classical Greek literature.
	1. Good people being ruined because of things that just happen to them, things they do not control.
	2. Good people doing bad things, things opposite of their ethical character and commitments because of things they do not control.
	3. Good people doing bad things without direct physical compulsion and opposite of their ethical character and commitments though done in light of circumstances of equally bad choices.

For instance, in ‘Agamemnon’, Agamemnon is waiting on the shore with his entire fleet. He intends to carry out a directive from Zeus to avenge his brother whose wife ran away with Paris, prince of Troy. However, because of an earlier transgression against Artemis, Artemis is holding back the wind, stranding his fleet. Agamemnon, as the result of an omen of an eagle devouring a hare, is told that in order to appease Artemis, he must sacrifice his daughter Iphigenia. As such, Agamemnon is faced with two impossible choices, fail to carry out Zeus’s command, or kill his daughter. It is important to point out that the fleet is already facing starvation being stranded on the coast. Ultimately, Agamemnon does make the choice to have his daughter murdered to appease Artemis. However, Agamemnon’s sin, is not that he choices sacrificing his daughter since it was an impossible choice, instead it is the way he sacrificed his daughter.

Agamemnon’s sin however, is not the choice, but rather how the choice was made. Nussbaum tells us. ‘Agamemnon now begins to cooperate inwardly with necessity, arranging his feelings to accord with his fortune.’ From the moment he makes his decision, though we are led to believe that it was the best he could have made, he becomes a willing collaborator, a willing victim [35].

Importantly, there is an important progression of loss of agency to its recapture:

- Necessity of the act
- to the rightness of the act
- to the rightness of the feelings of the act

with the specific point of recapture being with Agamemnon failed to morn his choice.

In another paper, I highlighted some concerns raised by Wendy Laybourn ([2018](http://localhost:6369/papers/paper-1-aes-evi/#ref-layb18)) regarding representation and authenticity. Here, I want to draw attention to some additional concerns surrounding the kind of knowledge Agamemnon lacked in the tale above. What is important here is that these criticisms unfairly focus too narrowly on supposed harms against blacks, and do not do a good enough job in recognizing that these as potentially universal harms. Given their universality, I argue that a best response is found in a Christian theodicy to evil as a form of suffering.

Wendy Laybourn ([2018](http://localhost:6369/papers/paper-1-aes-evi/#ref-layb18)) draws the comparison between black authenticity and what she calls *commodified realness*. Laybourn ([2018](http://localhost:6369/papers/paper-1-aes-evi/#ref-layb18))’s criticism of black authenticity and what she calls commodified realness is a commodified identity that is sold to a broader public as black authenticity. This criticism is centered around the effects of what she calls controlling images in popular media. Of deep concern here, is that many of the members of the communities depicted by such images consider aspects of these images to be authentic representations of themselves and the communities in which they live. and not in themselves harmful (E.g., [20C History Project 2014](http://localhost:6369/papers/paper-1-aes-evi/#ref-20c14); [Collins 2006](http://localhost:6369/papers/paper-1-aes-evi/#ref-coll06), [2009](http://localhost:6369/papers/paper-1-aes-evi/#ref-coll09); [Sartwell 1998](http://localhost:6369/papers/paper-1-aes-evi/#ref-sart98b)). Yet too, this is also a common trope.

I present this case as an example of a paradigmatic concept of hermeneutic injustice. It is not clear just what aspects of the member’s lives are represented, nor specifically which images are harmful if such images are accepted as representative. The worry then is that this kind of harm leads to the state Agamemnon found himself in. An important question here has to do with the relationship between popular narratives that contain these images, and this special kind of knowledge. One approach argues that we are defined by the stories we accept about ourselves, but this is controversial and it is this point I will address here.

# **The Virtue of Luck (a reading of luck and ethics from Fragility of Goodness)**

Here, I have characterized depictions of the manticore as a response to epistemic harms, most notably, harms that are hermeneutic and mitigate an person’s knowledge of the importance of relationship. These kinds of epistemic harms the medievalists worried about, and are not far off from contemporary epistemic harms and risks; misinformation, false and inauthentic narratives, and bullsh*t. This article began by looking at those beasts which represented epistemic harms more generally. It then developed an account of inauthentic narratives, narratives that miss their semantic marks. I argued what it would look like to recognize these as moral harms. What is now needed, a a deeper understanding of the relationship between human virtue and environmental variables. In order for a trait to be the virtue of a human being, some would like to say that it has to represent choices, rationality, actions, beliefs. However, often times these depend on environmental variables which we do not control. Here, depictions of the manticore would be appropriate seeing as how what is needed is a response to such environmental variables. Most importantly among these are the values that we hold dear to us. Whether or not we recognize the importance of close relationships for instance. However, if the luck question is accurate, even these values depend on factors that are outside of our control. Our fragility, need for loved ones, friendship, etc., can at one time depend on whether or not we’ve encountered sufficient hardships for instance, but even outside the experience of hardships, friendships, which are also external and an environmental variable hold intrinsic importance, although these are external to us. Additional work on this subject might include Robert de Fournival’s *Bestiare d’Amour *for instance. Important here is the concept that “human goodness is nourished, even constituted, by external happenings” [1-2] yet, this is impossible without the exercise of individual virtue.

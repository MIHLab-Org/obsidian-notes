        ## Country Music
        In their paper Outlaw Epistemologies: Resisting the viciousness of country music’s settler ignorance, Bryce Huebner and Shelbi Nahwilet Meissner develop two accounts of what they call epistemic scaffolding, a process through which the “user” constructs various identities. In their conclusion, Huebner and Meissner develop an account of country music that creates an “outlaw epistemology” whose object is to counter the effects of what they call “vicious country”, songs which “impose racialized, gendered, and sexualized presumptions onto indigenous people”.
        The response then is to create “outlaw epistemologies”.
> Of course, an outlaw epistemologist won’t stop here. Rather than letting country music reify the colonial bedrock, they will treat sedimented reality as scaffolding for epistemic chronostratigraphy, or trickster hermeneutics, and they will try to cultivate resistant epistemologies. . . . But whether you saddle up to take your horse down that old town road (Lil Nas X, 2019) or hope the reservation road will take you home (Stateline, 2004), you should always have tools for constructing more resistant and resilient epistemologies, and building richer structures for co-flourishing, in your holster.

        ## Philosophy of Religion
        Susan Pepper-Gates in a forth-coming paper writes a very engaging and important paper article applying Miranda Fricker (2007)’s analysis of epistemic injustice to contemporary Western Religion WR, and the Philosophy of Religion, PoWR. She argues that the philosophy of religion should cultivate epistemic-ethical virtues in order to recognize and include philosophers from minority voices. Doing so helps WR and PoWR understand and recognize the ways that power shapes our lives as embodied social beings. A point made by Pepper-Gates is that religion should be used as a method to counter the effects of harmful epistemologies. 
        In other words then, by including minority voices in discussions of religion, interested readers in the philosophy of religion will be able to develop a better appreciation for the ways that cultural leaders can shape the individual lives of the public. Pepper-Gates then makes the point that religion should be used as a method to counter the effects of harmful epistemologies.

        ### The Physiologus and its Contemporaries
        Bestiaries notably are medieval texts that stem from an earlier tradition in classical Greek literature, especially the animal taxonomies created by Aristotle and Ctesias. Sometime around 343 BCE, Aristotle and a student of his, Theophrastus, would found a school called the *Lyceum*. They would then go on to develop what are now known to be the biological sciences with Aristotle focusing treatises on the systematic reflections on animal anatomy, physiology, and their development with Theophrastus doing the same for plants.
        Of particular importance for the medieval ages, is Book II of Aristotle’s History of the Animals. However, these did not become the bestiaries we know of today. Rather, it was that until the production of the account known as the Physiologus, that the creatures we find in the bestiaries of the medieval period where merely scientific treatises, although incomplete in some ways. It was believed that these animals were thought to exist by their authors, or perhaps as in Aristotle’s case, reflects a disputation of the written record. The manticore is one such creature, of whom Aristotle disputes the written record. 
         On some accounts, the manticore is a union between a hyena and Ethiopian lioness. It imitates the voices of men and possesses a fixed and immoveable gaze. In addition, it does not possess gums in either of its jaws, and rather than having multiple teeth, it has one large one that curves around its jaw bone.
![[image 11.png|Manticore with a human head. Westminster Abbey Library. © Dean and Chapter of Westminster]]

        Pliny the elder describes the manticore as a union between an Ethiopian lioness and hyaena. Two notoriously deadly enemies who stage their battles across the African plain. The fact that he draws emphasis on the hyaena being male while the lioness is female suggests that the manticore is born from a procreative union between the two, and not merely a creative one. Perhaps the manticore has obtained its ability to speak from the male hyaena’s ability to imitate the voice of a man which it purportedly often used to draw the man away from the safety of his home where he could be devoured by the hyaena.

Pliny tells us that the hyaena is able to learn a man’s name, so that in calling him away from the protection of his home, can then devour him. It appears that the manticore too possesses this ability, and not only of men, but can imitate the voices of cattle in addition. Like Aristotle, Pliny claims that the manticore does not posses gums in either of its jaws and that its teeth are one continuous piece of bone.

Ctesias, writing earlier than Aristotle, provides a description that includes the Manticore having a triple row of teeth which fit into each other like a comb, the face and ears of a human, blood colored eyes, the body of a lion and a stinging tail similar to a scorpion. While Pliny the elder argued that it imitated the voices of men, Ctesias tells us that its voice resembles a union of a flute and trumpet, that it is exceedingly swift, and has an appetite for human flesh [p. 280].

Aristotle questions Ctesias’ account, but nevertheless preserves it in his own writings. He also reproduces Ctesias’ account, that the Manticore possesses a double rather than triple row of teeth, has similar feet as a lion and is similar in size to a lion. What is perhaps important are the anthropomorphic descriptions including those of the ears and face of a human, and that it has non-anthropomorphic ones such as grey eyes and red body and possesses a tail like that of a scorpion. [p. 30-31; also see Aristotle, History of Animals 8.28, 605b22-606a10]

Unlike classical inscriptions of creatures such as the manticore, when we talk about aesthetic function, it is often not an animal’s status as real or imaginary that is a central feature of the animal’s inclusion into the medieval bestiary. A marked change occurred via mentions of the manticore from the classical Greek period into the medieval ages. The medieval bestiary was not a biological taxonomy for the purposes of animal classification like in the Greek classical period. Rather, the animal’s inclusion into the bestiary is sometimes a purely aesthetic function where its descriptions are dependent in part on the functions they are intended to perform. However, it would not be entirely accurate to say that this function did not possess any truth values. Instead, this change is to expresses an emerging early and crude form of psychological science.

On the one hand, it may be tempting to say that the value of the animal’s inclusion is a symbolic gesture with pure formal aesthetic features, form beauty, etc. But often times the inclusion had Christological purposes, such as the representation of some important religious significance. Therefore,  the medieval bestiary in some respects was a devotional meant to glorify the creator ([Morrison and Grollemond 2019, 39](http://localhost:6369/papers/paper-1-aes-evi/#ref-morr19)). However my claim deviates somewhat from Morrison et al in that it concerns more practical interests dealing with issues of mind rather than post death body.  Sanctification is an attempted treatment to psychological ailments, of which only Christ constitutes a real cure.

There is a stark division in the kinds of reflections medieval representations of the manticore involved, as some animals represented Christian reflections of salvation whereas others represented threats to salvation. One such example includes images of a lion breathing on his dead cub and after three days reviving it just as Christ died on the cross and rose after three days ([Morrison and Grollemond 2019, 4](http://localhost:6369/papers/paper-1-aes-evi/#ref-morr19)).

According to some hermeneutic views centering on epistemic injustice, because of a misappropriated power imbalance, a person holds false beliefs about themselves. These beliefs stem from unjust environments where the individual lacks access to resources that would lead to accurate self-understanding and moral beliefs. Such beliefs are rooted in irrational actions performed by that individual. One such concept of hermeneutic justice has been popularized by Miranda Fricker ([2007](http://localhost:6369/papers/paper-1-aes-evi/#ref-fric07)) though it was also developed by earlier feminists. As an artistic object, the Manticore is a fictionalized beast that was born in Persian art and literature sometime around its cultural zenith. It at once symbolizes evil, death and hell, but in medieval religious art, it had come to symbolize the power of Christ over death and hell.

We might think that medieval art has no place in a discussion about epistemic injustice, but the depiction of the fictional beast called the manticore, as a personification of deception lends credibility to the claim that medieval art is an early representation of the dangers of epistemic harms, an early representation of the extent to which early authorities recognized epistemic harms. According to the modern function of art summed up by Gilmore ([2020](http://localhost:6369/papers/paper-1-aes-evi/#ref-gilm20)), art’s function is oriented to ends such as pleasure, entertainment, and absorption [132]. Conversely however, and perhaps an emerging consideration, includes the medieval function of art as directed at more orthodox ends, intending to motivate positive emotion states, or desirable behaviors and behavioral responses to repressive environmental stimuli.

In medieval bestiaries, there are two classes of evil represented by beasts; those which deceive and those which generate fear and death. Among the first class of beasts, those which are notable for their abilities to deceive, there are three kinds of deception that cause epistemic harms to the targets: these are noted by the camouflages of the chameleon, accurate imitations of the manticore, and the “seductive skills of sirens”. 

- Deceivers: 	Camouflage, imitation, seduction 	
	- Chameleon: camouflage 
	-  Manticore: accurate 	imitations 
	-  Sirens: seductive skills 
-  Fear and Death Generators 

While we might think this to be a medieval taxonomy of epistemic harms only, the kinds of epistemic harms the medievalists worried about are not far off from contemporary epistemic harms and risks; misinformation, false and inauthentic narratives, and bullsh*t. This article looks at those beasts which represent epistemic harms and attempts to develop an account of inauthentic narrative as a moral harm recognized by church leaders in the Middle Ages.

**Some Questions**

A central question here is one of difference in kinds of harm. Specifically what kind of harm is being done. Additionally, is the kind of harm that we care about made problematic solely in virtue of racial, or ethnic distinctiveness or can we understand expositions on epistemic harm to be instead warnings of falling prey to specific kinds of epistemic harms? 

## What is an epistemic harm?

Peppers-Gates first points out that Western Religion, WR, and The Philosophy of Western Religion, PoWR, have historically ignored work by intersectional feminist philosophers and philosophers of race who challenge the traditional approach. However, how is this done? First, traditional epistemology, TE, focuses on abstract universal knowledge while the philosophy of religion has traditionally grounded itself in TE by focusing on rational belief.

Therefore, in order for the philosophy of religion to cultivate epistemic-ethical virtues, it should recognize and include philosophers from minority voices. This argument takes the following form:

1. Traditional epistemology focuses on abstract universal knowledge.
2. Philosophy of religion grounds itself in traditional epistemology and focuses on rational belief.
3. Therefore WR and PoWR embrace narrow methodologies that exclude others and contributes to the marginalization of minority voices.
4. Given its focus on abstract universal knowledge and its marginalization of minority voices, WR and PoWR ignore work by intersectional feminist philosophers and philosophers of race who challenge the traditional approach and argue that power shapes our lives as embodied social beings.
5. As such, WR and PoWR conflate the truth about the existence and attributes of a [patriarchal] God with traditional epistemic approaches grounded in [narrow methodological approaches to knowledge].
6. By focusing on narrow methodologies and abstract conceptions of knowledge, WR and PoWR fail to consider ways that power shapes our lives as embodied social beings.

A central point in the above argument is that WR and PoWR have silenced marginalized groups. These include women, people of color, disabled persons and members of the LGBTQI+ community. As stated above, the reason for this is because traditional epistemology focuses on abstract conceptions of knowledge and thereby fails to consider the ways that power shapes our lives as embodied social beings. We might think however, that the argument rests on a notion of rules bias. Namely that by using certain conversational rules in philosophical discourse, that PoWR and WR silence minority groups due to their falling in some way to abide by these rules of discourse, or that such rules are not applicable to these communities.

To see how this is the case, we can use the grammar contrast. Here, we assume that a person can learn grammar and we exclude them from the larger conversation until they do. But this would be an unfair response to the above argument. On the one hand, we could argue that it is grounded in a misreading of the argument, that minority voices are excluded from the philosophical lexicon not because they have failed to abide by the rules of philosophical discourse, but because either 1) their topics of concern fall outside the margin of interest, or 2) because of some varying identifying physical characteristic, such as failure to develop a ‘rational’ argument as determined by the implied narrow rules of discourse. But this would be a misrepresentation of the above argument.

The way I understand Peppers-Gates argument, is that marginalized groups have been excluded from philosophical discourse because of certain hermeneutic challenges, namely the way they are *understood* as having failed to abide by the rules of philosophical discourse. The identifying characteristic prevents philosophical audiences from recognizing the way minority voices do develop ‘rational’ argument according to the above defined narrow rules of discourse. Therefore, we can amend the above argument in the following way:

7. TE focuses on abstract conceptions of knowledge.
8. PoWR grounds itself in TE by focusing on ‘rational’ belief in its debates about God’s existence and attributes.
9. Therefore PoWR ignores feminist philosophers and philosophers of race who challenge PoWR’s rules which are grounded in TE.
10. As such, WR and PoWR have silenced marginal groups.
11. PoWR’s embrace of its narrow methodology and range of topics then is based in an exclusion of ‘others’ from meaning making in institutional philosophy.

The final premise then says that the narrow methodology employed by PoWR is not a natural methodology, but is rather exclusive in its construction. Meaning that it is narrow specifically for the purposes of excluding minority views and opinions within the lexicon. As such, I understand that the paper defends the following conclusion:

12. The Philosophy of Western Religion focuses on rational belief and conflates this with evolving and misapplied lexical rules in its debates about the existence and attributes of a patriarchal God.

I disagree with 3. Instead, there is a case to be made that says minority voices are excluded from the philosophical discourse when their topics of concern fall outside of establishment interests.

## **Silencing**

According to the argument, western religion and the philosophy of religion have silenced marginalized groups by focusing on abstract conceptions of knowledge which fail to consider ways that power shapes our lives as embodied social beings. This is because traditional epistemology focuses on abstract conceptions of knowledge. These abstract conceptions of knowledge are inadequate because they do not analyze the ways power shapes our lives as “embodied social beings”. We are born in webs of meaning and social relations of power and privilege. Some voices are heard, meaning they pass review, and therefore shape the social imagination. Others are ignored however. These others then are incapable of shaping the social imagination.

On the one hand, a defense of the traditional power structure would make sure to emphasize what it means to participate in the sort of debate that does have the potential to shape the social imagination. How is one’s voice heard? One way is through the enforcement of so-called grammar rules. Taking an art class, the professor attempts to communicate the rules which apply to a given art genre. However, the students note the many deviations from these rules, the supposed exceptions.

The professor then replies by saying that “once you know how to apply the rules, then and only then are you allowed to break the rules”. An possible analogy would be a reviewer rejecting a paper for the alleged abuse of grammar rules that a more seasoned author would be allowed to bend. A concern here is one where the so-called rules are being inappropriately applied as a means of gate-keeping who gets to shape the ‘social-imagination’. As such then, it is probably not correct that a person’s work gets rejected for not following the agreed upon grammatical rules, but rather they are being accused of trying to change the rules.

While a student who fails to learn the rules, is not accepted into the art community, where work is posted in galleries, sold at auctions, is publicly (positively or negatively) reviewed etc., it is not that our student has failed to learn the rules, but rather that the student has failed to give sufficient attention to topics of importance. Of additional importance here, is that assuming a more seasoned author is justified in bending the lexical rules of philosophical discourse, than the more seasoned author can also be seen as contributing to the development of the lexicon rules surrounding philosophical discourse. As such, the more seasoned professional is allowed to shape the rules of discourse, and thereby contribute to the social imagination.

The harm which should concern us here includes the exclusion of individuals from the generally shared social understanding. Excluding voices in this way contributes to their harm or can be used as a weapon to harm. But what is this weapon and how specifically does it as a weapon cause harm? Following this, their capacity as a knower and own self-understanding have been stunted. But this introduces a very narrow vision of social structure. Not necessarily.

The need to reimagine education begins with a stated purpose that goes beyond the simple transmission of basic fact and includes developing character. This vision of the kind of person that you want to be contributes to your capacity as a knower, and fortifies your self-understanding. So while a person is excluded from a dss, they could have their capacity as a knower fortified by a non-dss. # **Patricia Collins Black Feminist Thought**

In the 2009 version of Collin’s Black Feminist Thought, BFT, Collins draws greater emphasis between relationships of knowledge and power. It is often said

- that knowledge = power,

but a worrying theme throughout the book, is that this is wrong and that rather

- power = knowledge.

One might notice the misapplication of a logical principle here, because whenever we use the equals sign, there is not a logical difference between power = knowledge and knowledge = power.

> Rather, this says that power is identical in value to knowledge and if power is identical to knowledge, then knowledge is identical to power.

In other words, the initial application claims that the direction of fit is not directional but rather than uni- or bi-directional.

For instance, we could look at some possible relationships between knowledge and power below:

- knowledge $\rightarrow$ power

or

- power $\rightarrow$ knowledge

or

- knowledge $\leftrightarrow$ power

Initially, we might think that assuming that if one has knowledge, then the correct state of affairs should be that they will fulfill their aims, whatever those may be. Ricardo Piñero Moral (2021a) deviates somewhat from the norm in his analysis of medieval bestiaries. There are many interesting topics of discussion surrounding the creation of bestiaries. These include the depictions of humans and their interaction with exotic and fantastic animals as the creators of a given bestiary would have imagined, the gold leaf that is sometimes used on the edges of their pages, and the sometimes-comical stories their pictures depict. Rather than focusing on their visual imagery, Moral seeks to develop an account of the meaning which the medieval artists and authors sought to uncover in his analysis of their contents. More importantly, he focuses on the harm expressed as fantastic beasts. Even more importantly still, he focuses on the warnings of evil in the medieval mind. But there are two ways to understand evil and assuming that the medievalist is being more practical then many modern historians might give him credit for, then we can easily translate evil as harm.


As such, Moral’s paper is a valid way to begin the discussion given the topic of this collection. Notable among Moral’s aims in his discussion of harm is deception and fear. Looking at harms grounded in a kind of deception, what is important here is that the three beasts associated with deception are also associated with pleasure. The relationship here is obvious. It is a relationship from a promise of pleasure to the end of the flourishing of the victim, the harm. The two specific animals he considers are the manticore which commits the sin of imitation while the siren plays the seductress.


Bestiaries notably are medieval texts that stem from an earlier tradition in classical Greek literature, especially the animal taxonomies created by Aristotle and Ctesias. On some accounts, the manticore is a union between a hyena and Ethiopian lioness. It imitates the voices of men and possesses a fixed and immoveable gaze. In addition, it does not possess gums in either of its jaws, and rather than having multiple teeth, it has one large one that curves around its jawbone.


Pliny the elder is one who describes the manticore as a union between an Ethiopian lioness and hyaena. Two notoriously deadly enemies who stage their battles across the African plain. The fact that he draws emphasis on the hyaena being male while the lioness is female suggests that the manticore is born from a procreative union between the two, and not merely a creative one. Perhaps the manticore has obtained its ability to speak from the male hyaena’s ability to imitate the voice of a man. Pliny tells us that the hyaena is able to learn a man’s name, so that calling him away from the protection of his home can then devour him. It appears that the manticore too possesses this ability, and not only of men, but can imitate the voices of cattle in addition. Like Aristotle, Pliny claims that the manticore does not possess gums in either of its jaws, and that its teeth are one continuous piece of bone.


Ctesias’ description includes the Manticore having a triple row of teeth which fit into each other like a comb, the face and ears of a human, blood-colored eyes, the body of a lion and a stinging tail like a scorpion. While Pliny the elder argued that it imitated the voices of men, Ctesias tells us that its voice resembles a union of a flute and trumpet, that it is exceedingly swift, and has an appetite for human flesh [p. 280].

Unlike classical inscriptions of creatures such as the manticore, when we talk about aesthetic function, it is often not an animal’s status as real or imaginary that is a central feature of the animal’s inclusion into the medieval bestiary. A marked change occurred via mentions of the manticore from the classical Greek period into the medieval ages. The medieval bestiary was not a biological taxonomy for the purposes of animal classification like in the Greek classical period. Rather, the animal’s inclusion into the bestiary is sometimes a purely aesthetic function where its descriptions are dependent in part on the functions they are intended to perform. However, it would not be entirely accurate to say that this function did not possess any truth values. Instead, this change is to expresses an emerging early and crude form of psychological science.

On the one hand, it may be tempting to say that the value of the animal’s inclusion is a symbolic gesture with pure formal aesthetic features, form beauty, etc. But often times the inclusion had Christological purposes, such as the representation of some important religious derivation. Therefore,  medieval bestiary in some respects was a devotional meant to glorify the creator ([Morrison and Grollemond 2019, 39](http://localhost:6369/papers/paper-1-aes-evi/#ref-morr19)). However my claim deviates somewhat from Morrison et al in that it concerns more practical interests dealing with issues of mind rather than post death body.

There is a stark division in the kinds of reflections medieval representations of the manticore involved, as some animals represented Christian reflections of salvation whereas others represented threats to salvation. One such example includes images of a lion breathing on his dead cub and after three days reviving it just as Christ died on the cross and rose after three days ([Morrison and Grollemond 2019, 4](http://localhost:6369/papers/paper-1-aes-evi/#ref-morr19)). Another point of stark contrast includes animals as the representation of either virtues or vices that are thought to be common to human experience. Many of the animal descriptions found in the bestiary are derived initially from Pliny the Elder’s reflections on classical animal taxonomies. However, many of the many of the extant versions of the bestiary derive from the Greek Physiologus probably written by one of the patristic fathers sometime before 496 AD when it was translated into Latin (Beer, Jeanette M. A. 2003. *Beasts of Love: Richard de Fournival’s Bestiaire d’amour and A Woman’s Response*. University of Toronto Press., 8).

There are several ways in which some art objects may represent more complex realities. One may think that the purpose of a painting is to reflect the experiences one had during a war, or peace time event. Such a painting may express the given facts of the case, for instance, an artist could paint the signing of the Magna Carta. If the painting were painted by an artist was a personal witness to the occasion, then the artist would merely be representing contemporary (for them) reality. If the artist were not alive at the time, then we could argue that the artist is representing historical reality. Another distinction includes the artist relying on trusted and well known sources as this would be different from the artist imagining an event in which not many direct sources exist. But an artist can represent a projection of reality as well, when the reality they want to convey is obscure, not well known, highly contested or hypothetical. An excellent example of this is Richard de Fournival's *Bestiaire d'amour’.*

Piñero Moral ([2021b](http://localhost:6369/papers/paper-1-aes-evi/#ref-mora21)) argues that art’s purpose in the middle ages is to represent reality in an even more complex, especially moral reality. de Fournival in his *Bestiaire d’amour* at once makes a declaration of love while at the same time, acting in his ecclesiastical role, attacks it. One example of medieval representations of moral reality include the Persian Manticore. The manticore as depicted in medieval art is said to represent evil. According the this definition of medieval art, art implies metaphysical aspirations and normative elements (Also see [De Bruyne 1959](http://localhost:6369/papers/paper-1-aes-evi/#ref-debr59)). Its commitment to reality is undeniable, yet the reality which it attempts to represent is so complex and vast that the limits between what is and what is not, between what we believe and what we know, between what we are and what we wish to be, are often blurred ([Cronin 1941](http://localhost:6369/papers/paper-1-aes-evi/#ref-cron41)). Using the bestiary, Richard is both able to develop a kind of pedagogy with two divergent aims, to teach of the nature of love, his *declaration*, while guard his audience against it.

Using the bestiary, the artist can represent facts as they believe them to be, or as they wish them to be. It is this latter motivation that Pinero suggests motivated medieval artwork representing the manticore. There is a difference here between modern and medieval artistic function. This in itself is uninteresting. However, what may be more interesting to the reader, is the further claim that this difference can be applied in response to the contemporary account of epistemic injustice.

Owing to the fantastical nature of these accounts, it is highly probable that they are either misguided representations of some more familiar animal, or motivated by purposes other than truth. In the classical era, it is most likely the case that the Manticore is a confused representation of some other more familiar animal, while in the medieval period, the motivation for the representation lies outside of truth. We might think that medieval art has no place in a discussion about epistemic injustice, but the depiction of the fictional beast called the manticore, as a personification of deception lends credibility to the claim that medieval art is an early representation of the dangers of epistemic harms. According to the modern function of art summed up by Gilmore ([2020](http://localhost:6369/papers/paper-1-aes-evi/#ref-gilm20)), art’s function is oriented to ends such as pleasure, entertainment, and absorption [132]. Conversely, the medieval function of art is directed at more orthodox ends intending to motivate positive emotion states, or desirable behaviors and behavioral responses.

According to some hermeneutic views centering on epistemic injustice, because of a misappropriated power imbalance, a person holds false beliefs about themselves. These beliefs stem from unjust environments where the individual lacks access to resources that would lead to accurate self-understanding and moral beliefs. Such beliefs are rooted in irrational actions performed by that individual. One such concept of hermeneutic justice has been popularized by Miranda Fricker ([2007](http://localhost:6369/papers/paper-1-aes-evi/#ref-fric07)) though it was also developed by earlier feminists. As an artistic object, the Manticore is a fictionalized beast that was born in Persian art and literature sometime around its cultural zenith. It at once symbolizes evil, death and hell, but in medieval religious art, it had come to symbolize the power of Christ over death and hell.

# **On Greek Tragedy**

As I’ve mentioned before, the classical world often treats narrative texts differently than the modern world. However, Martha Nussbaum in her book ‘The Fragility of Goodness’ has defended a novel interpretation of what we might think to be modern aesthetic practice found in classical Greek literature. She first notes three important sources of narrative conflict in classical Greek literature.

27. Good people being ruined because of things that just happen to them, things they do not control.
28. Good people doing bad things, things opposite of their ethical character and commitments because of things they do not control.
29. Good people doing bad things without direct physical compulsion and opposite of their ethical character and commitments though done in light of circumstances of equally bad choices.

For instance, in ‘Agamemnon’, Agamemnon is waiting on the shore with his entire fleet. He intends to carry out a directive from Zeus to avenge his brother whose wife ran away with Paris, prince of Troy. However, because of an earlier transgression against Artemis, Artemis is holding back the wind, stranding his fleet. Agamemnon, as the result of an omen of an eagle devouring a hare, is told that in order to appease Artemis, he must sacrifice his daughter Iphigenia. As such, Agamemnon is faced with two impossible choices, fail to carry out Zeus’s command, or kill his daughter. It is important to point out that the fleet is already facing starvation being stranded on the coast. Ultimately, Agamemnon does make the choice to have his daughter murdered to appease Artemis. However, Agamemnon’s sin, is not that he choices sacrificing his daughter since it was an impossible choice, instead it is the way he sacrificed his daughter.

Agamemnon’s sin however, is not the choice, but rather how the choice was made. Nussbaum tells us. ‘Agamemnon now begins to cooperate inwardly with necessity, arranging his feelings to accord with his fortune.’ From the moment he makes his decision, though we are led to believe that it was the best he could have made, he becomes a willing collaborator, a willing victim [35].

Importantly, there is an important progression of loss of agency to its recapture:

- Necessity of the act
- to the rightness of the act
- to the rightness of the feelings of the act

with the specific point of recapture being with Agamemnon failed to morn his choice.

# **The Virtue of Luck (a reading of luck and ethics from Fragility of Goodness)**

Here, I have characterized depictions of the manticore as a response to epistemic harms, most notably, harms that are hermeneutic and mitigate an person’s knowledge of the importance of relationship. These kinds of epistemic harms the medievalists worried about, and are not far off from contemporary epistemic harms and risks; misinformation, false and inauthentic narratives, and bullsh*t. This article began by looking at those beasts which represented epistemic harms more generally. It then developed an account of inauthentic narratives, narratives that miss their semantic marks. I argued what it would look like to recognize these as moral harms. What is now needed, a a deeper understanding of the relationship between human virtue and environmental variables. In order for a trait to be the virtue of a human being, some would like to say that it has to represent choices, rationality, actions, beliefs. However, often times these depend on environmental variables which we do not control. Here, depictions of the manticore would be appropriate seeing as how what is needed is a response to such environmental variables. Most importantly among these are the values that we hold dear to us. Whether or not we recognize the importance of close relationships for instance. However, if the luck question is accurate, even these values depend on factors that are outside of our control. Our fragility, need for loved ones, friendship, etc., can at one time depend on whether or not we’ve encountered sufficient hardships for instance, but even outside the experience of hardships, friendships, which are also external and an environmental variable hold intrinsic importance, although these are external to us.

Decision theory derives from a long tradition in consequentialism and epistemology. Philosophers are often concerned with normative questions about how people should decide to act rather than descriptive ones about how they in fact do decide to act. However, many authors have pointed out that normative decision theory cannot always guide us through important decisions (LA Paul 2015a; 2015b; Rust, 2025; Carter 2026). Paul (2015a; 2015b) inaccessible experiences with personally transformative ones. Others have argued that normative decision theories are impossible because we often have imperfect access to our own epistemic states. (cf. Jackson 1991; Howard-Snyder 1997; Lenman 2000). I highlight a special class of normative decisions. While such decisions may not be practically decided on, being in love versus not being in love, they may be narratively decided on (Rust 2025). Using Stump 2010, I then will argue that another's stories of suffering can teach us about values we might have that would otherwise be inaccessible to us. In other words, that a rational decision theory then may be improved by a narrative decision theory of rational choice.

Orthodox decision theory then, recommends acting in a way that maximizes actual or expected value. However our credences with regard to what we value, are opaque and therefore we cannot identify those acts which maximize actual or expected value (Carter 2026; (cf. Smith 2010; Srinivasan 2015; Lasonen-Aarnio 2019;2024; Lasonen 2025; Hughes 2021).. Some decisions require such access however. 

The implication here is that we should always choose to do that action that we believe will produce the most value. However, “doing whatever will provide the most value” is often hard [See Lewis, 1981; also see Carter; 2026; Gallow 2021; and Isaacs *et al* 2023; also see Smith 2010.]. It is argued that we do not have access to the world in a way that would allow us to follow through on our plans to maximize value (especially see, Gallow, 2021, p. 3). 

This is because we have uneven evidence in that our confidence towards genuine personal states of value are often higher than our confidence towards illusory personal states of value (Lasonen-Aarnio 2015; Salow 2018; Isaacs and Russell 2023). One outcome in such cases, is that it is often easier to be confident that we are in love, when we are in fact in love, than it is to be confident that we are out of love, when we are in fact, not in love. We often assign equal credence towards states of value we are in whether genuine or illusory (Lasonen-Aarnio 2015; Salow 2018; Isaacs and Russell 2023). An opaque decision problem represents the situation of an agent who must decide what to do while being (potentially) uncertain about their own credences (Carter, 2026, 3).

We might follow some plan of action, only to find out that the promised outcome is not what we desired after all. Such mistakes in judgment then, stem from failures to recognize our own priorities. Therefore standard normative decision theories cannot always guide us through important decisions (LA Paul 2015a; 2015b; Carter 2026) and decision theory proposes an answer. 

There are many versions of this concern. For instance, let’s imagine that on the basis of a coinflip, we will either be shown a genuine or a counterfeit Rothko. If we are shown the counterfeit but not the genuine version, we would be less likely to question its authenticity having no belief about the real one. We assign equal credence to either possibility, that the Rotko before us a genuine or a fake. Therefore, we might mistakenly think that upon looking at a counterfeit Rothko, that we desire to bid on the counterfeit Rothko rather than a genuine one (Carter, 2026, p. 9). What this means is that in the Rothko case, we have no reason to assume that the one we are bidding on is a counterfeit since it is the only one. This shows a different version of the problem, where we have no reason to value a different state of affairs since there is only one facing us.

The kind of common mistake that we make, which is important for Stump’s thesis, is that an opaque decision problem with respect to decision theory concerns special personal relationships. If we are confronted with two alternative versions of ourselves, then we would have more reason to question whether one is genuine or not. Stump argues that narratives give us reason to question whether one life, rather than any other, is genuine. I think that the best example of this fact can be represented with the following lyric excerpts from the songs “By Yourself” by Tyrone Griffin Jr (Ty Dollar $ign Diggs) and “Rich and Sad” by Austin Richard Post (Post Malone).

### The Nature of Love and Suffering

Using Stump 2010, I will argue that a rational decision theory then may be improved by a narrative decision theory of rational choice. 

Eleonore Stump defends a two-part Thomistic account of personal identity. A person’s first-order desires reflect the _reasons_ she has to take something as good whereas their second-order desires reflect the kind of will they want to have. She then contrasts a metaphysics of personal identity which gives a special focus to the rational faculties that a person possesses, where a person’s attribution of value is correlated with some particular action in some particular circumstance and a psychology of personal identity where a person’s attribution of value is correlated with general reasons of action grounded in their value of a good will.

Shame is the connection between moral wrongdoing and some defect of the will since it undermines our ability to experience fruitful and deep relationships with others. If I am guilty of stealing from a friend out of necessity, it will be difficult for me to face that individual and our relationship will be strained. If I desire union with the other person, then I will feel shame given that I expect rejection and abandonment by them. Over time however, I may not feel shame any longer. This is because I will no longer desire a relationship with that person.

The remedy may be to repair the will, but sometimes it is not possible for a person's will to change. Notice here that the temptation to steal from the friend came by way of perceived necessity, and therefore outside of my control. This highlights the difference between shame and guilt. I will feel guilt out of my anticipation to be the object of anger or punishment whereas I experience shame when I am anticipating abandonment or rejection. I experience shame out of my desire to be loved by others and conviction that something about me warrants their rejection of me. Therefore, because the perceived infraction was outside of my control, repairing the shame too is outside of my control.

The remedy for shame rests in the need of paired processes of justification and sanctification. Importantly, as a response to the problem of evil, Stump recommends an account of friendship for psychological well-being, where this friendship is grounded in union with God.

1. There is suffering in the world.
2. There is an omniscient, omnipotent, perfectly good God.
3. There is no morally sufficient reason for an omniscient, omnipotent, perfectly good God to allow suffering in the world.

A problem with this account, is that it rests on theological commitment. However, it in itself is not sufficient for justifying theistic beliefs. As a response to the problem of evil, it suffers because it needs a defense of theistic belief. But perhaps a better way to understand it is teleological. A path forward, is to think of the response as an definition of suffering. What is bad about suffering is that it undermines or destroys what the sufferer centrally cares about, whether they realize it or not.

* her own flourishing
* the desires of her heart
* or both

#### Standard Decision Formulas

Rational choice theory says "choose the act with the highest expected utility." This results in us having uneven evidence in that our confidence towards genuine personal states of value are often higher than our confidence towards illusory personal states of value (Lasonen-Aarnio 2015; Salow 2018; Isaacs and Russell 2023).  While however, such decisions may not be practically decided on, being in love versus not being in love, they may be narratively decided on (Rust 2025). 

According to rational decision theory, the expected utility of some action depends of the probability that the state of affairs which obtains, is the relevant one. However, complicating this, is the probability that the outcome would be considered valuable by the agent.

When confronted with personally transformative decisions, we often have imperfect access to our own epistemic states (Carter, 2026; cf. Jackson 1991; Howard-Snyder 1997; Lenman 2000). Therefore we must often make decisions under conditions of uncertainty. But this uncertainty includes:

* Probability the agent will choose act *a*
* Probability the agent will find outcome *o* valuable.

The expected value of an action at some state $s$, is the sum of values of the outcomes produced by that action at each state (Carter, 2026, p. 4). We weight the sum of values according to the probability that is assigned to that state from the perspective of that state (Carter, 2026, p. 4). A plan to maximize expected value then recommends, in each state, one of the acts which has optimal expected value at that state.

The way an agent acts depends on the plan they are trying to follow. However, given uneven evidence, agents like us who are susceptible to error do not always act according to what their plan recommends for the state they are in. Where any state $s, [s]$ represents an agent whose act is determined according to what their plan recommends for $s$ and an enriched state, $(s, [s’])$ represents the circumstances of an agent who is in s but whose act is determined according to what their plan recommends for $s’$. Sets of states in the enriched state space can be thought of as propositions which can tell us about:

1. what the world is like
2. what the agent will act as if the world is like.

Evidence to the contrary is the kind of evidence a person needs that what they desire is not in fact what they desire. A special component of this, is the fact that the evidence we need, evidence to the contrary, is not always available to us. We can imagine some agent that wants to believe some proposition _p_ about themselves, for instance that they desire not a meaningful relationship, but a pricey car, but for all _p_, -_p_ is not a part of the agent’s evidence. For all the agent knows, desires consistent with desiring meaningful relationships, are not a part of their evidence set. In this case, an agent has evidence consistent with desiring a pricey car, solely for the reason that evidence consistent with desiring meaningful relationship, is not a part of their evidence set. This is because they may not have access to the necessary counter evidence, that they in fact, desire a meaningful relationship.

Our errors in judgment are often grounded in these more fundamental errors. Another similar example is that we are not vulnerable to love or that we are invulnerable to love. What this means is that a person believes about themselves, that they are not of the sort who “is mushy” or “terribly affectionate”, or that they are an extreme introvert, better off alone, etc. Perhaps this person may believe that they can thrive on short-lived, shallow relationships, or just as commonly, no relationship at all.

This has implications for the kinds of decisions that we make. Examples like the one above illustrate a kind of decision problem in which an agent will not expect to do best by trying to follow a plan to maximize expected value. We make a decision thinking that we desire a given outcome. This can include for instance, that we may experience more joy later on by breaking up with our fiancé now because they demand too much of our time. Our time here being a direct and measurable result that can be quantified, for instance to answer the question “how much did I get done today?” On the other hand however, if we spent the day with a loved one, it might be less obvious how to answer such a question according to quantifiable metrics.

Self 1: “How much did I get done today?”

Self 2: “Not much, I just spent the day with my loved one”.

Given our relationship with the other, “Not much” and “I just spent the day with my loved one” seem like contradictory statements. The more likely cause of this contradiction is that it is difficult to quantify one’s time spent in this manner, but that is not the same as it is to say that it is difficult to qualify one’s time spent in this manner. It may just be that rational choice theory does not have a way to qualify one’s time spent even though it has a way to quantify one’s time spent.

Often it turns out that the outcome that we thought we desired, was not the outcome that we desired should we achieve it. So while it would have been perhaps possible that the agent could have looked for evidence _that $\neg p$_, _**intentionally-biased inquiry, for instance, th**at maybe I am trauma bonding_, intentionally biased inquiry may be impossible [The Externalist’s Guide to Fishing for Compliments](https://app.notion.com/p/The-Externalist-s-Guide-to-Fishing-for-Compliments-3a38935bcf8a81c790eeca5e35bbdddc?pvs=21). 

Eleonore Stump has argued that love is the interaction of two desires, the first for the good of the beloved and the second, union with the beloved. However, a person often suffers in that they are divided against themselves and so cannot experience desire for the good of the other, or desire for union with another. Desiring the good of, and union with, another places us into a vulnerable state and it is not always clear whether vulnerability in this way is a virtue. 

There is a lot of disagreement over what kinds of things we are, whether relationally vulnerable wherein we desire to be with another, or relationally invulnerable where the outcome of not being with another is not a value that we possess, or that it is not a value that overrides other values that we possess. Additionally, _internal integration of the will_ would be necessary for love and union with another only if we are relationally vulnerable (Thomas Aquinas, “Adoro devote,” in _The Oxford Book of Medieval Latin Verse,_ ed. F.J.E. Raby (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1959), 403-4).

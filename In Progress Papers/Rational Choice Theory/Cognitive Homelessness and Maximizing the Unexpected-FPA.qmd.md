Decision theory derives from a long tradition in consequentialism “If, then, there is some end of things doable in action that we wish for because of itself, and the others because of it” [Nichomachean Ethics Bk I, chapter 2]. Noncausal rational choice theories do not make mention of an agent's credences as a factor in their decision making. Orthodox theory, attempts to improve on decision theory. When confronted with personally transformative decisions, we often have imperfect access to our own epistemic states (Carter, 2026; cf. Jackson 1991; Howard-Snyder 1997; Lenman 2000). Therefore we must often make decisions under conditions of uncertainty.

The expected value of an action at some state $s$, is the sum of values of the outcomes produced by that action at each state (Carter, 2026, p. 4). We weight the sum of values according to the probability that is assigned to that state from the perspective of that state (Carter, 2026, p. 4). A plan to maximize expected value then recommends, in each state, one of the acts which has optimal expected value at that state.

The way an agent acts depends on the plan they are trying to follow. However, agents like us who are susceptible to error do not always act according to what their plan recommends for the state they are in. Rather we can understand their susceptibility to err in terms of their underlying pattern of dispositions. To represent these dispositions, we need to represent the state an agent is acting as if they are in, what we can call “enriched state spaces”.

Where any state $s, [s]$ represents an agent whose act is determined according to what their plan recommends for $s$ and an enriched state, $(s, [s’])$ represents the circumstances of an agent who is in s but whose act is determined according to what their plan recommends for $s’$. Sets of states in the enriched state space can be thought of as propositions which can tell us about:

1. what the world is like
2. what the agent will act as if the world is like.

Evidence to the contrary is the kind of evidence a person needs that what they desire is not in fact what they desire. A special component of this, is the fact that the evidence we need, evidence to the contrary, is not always available to us.

We can imagine some agent that wants to believe some proposition _p_ about themselves, for instance that they are in love, but for all _p_, -_p_ is not a part of the agent’s evidence. For all the agent knows, desires consistent with not being in love either are, or are not, a part of their evidence set, not accessible to them as evidence. In this case, an agent has evidence consistent with being in love, solely for the reason that evidence consistent with not being in love, is not a part of their evidence set. This is because they may not have access to the necessary counter evidence, that they are, in fact, not in love.

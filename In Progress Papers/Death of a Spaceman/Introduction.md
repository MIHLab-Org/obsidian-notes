## How to use this template

This book is intended to be used along side a course in philosophy. Helen De Cruz once answered why it can be beneficial flourish philosophical arguments with detailed imaginative scripts. It may seem that imaginative flourishes on simple items like cocktail drinks, are unnecessary, but we do it any way. What is the value for instance, of adding a pink umbrella and three cherries to a martini (De Cruz et al. 2021)? Likewise, what might be the value of drawing a reader's focus to the strong beating heart of a victim tied to a train track, or of an expectant mother working twelve-hour shifts in a dark, damp mine? While traditional philosophy cultivates "lucidity, analysis, careful distinction, and rigorous argument", its focus is often times too narrow to be much good for the kinds of "complex, nuanced thought" many answers need (Stump 2010, 23–25).

This book is organized around game-based narrative thought experiments. Some might ask "what the point of roleplaying a thought experiment? Just lay out your philosophical position straight." If narratives can cultivate the kinds of complex, nuanced thought, then it can also help us to better understand behaviors, including our own. It follows the organizational principle used by David Chalmers in his book *Reality+*. Chalmers organizes the study of philosophy into three primary questions. These include "What's real? How do we know? Why should we care?" 

 **Organizing spine.** A major foundation for this book however is grounded in the final question, why we should care, and primarily then, it is a defense of the kinds of questions that characterize not only philosophy, but also religion. As such, when asking question like "what do we know?", the book's organizational principle is grounded in the importance of reflection for its own sake, as an intrinsic good. This book therefore will be a defense of philosophical reflection for the average reader. It begins with a exploration of wonder at the start, and bigger questions at the end. In addition, because the book is organized around questions of value, for instance the value of philosophy, the value of faith, etc., it also includes extensions into art and other explorations of beauty as goods that draw our attention to God.

**Chapter anatomy (copy this block for any new chapter):**

1. **Big Question** — one sentence, phrased the way a student would ask it.
2. **Editor's introduction** — [1,000–1,500 words, your prose]. Sets up the problem, names the interlocutor, previews the readings.
3. **Primary readings** — 2–4 selections, each with a headnote [150–250 words] and suggested page range.
4. **Contemporary bridge** — the matching _Reality+_ chapter(s).
5. **Lab** — the in-game thought experiment, written to the four-step experiment structure from your syllabus (claim → intuition → experiment → finding).
6. **Reconstruct the argument** — one argument from the readings for students to set out as premises and conclusion.
7. **Key terms** — 6–10 terms.
8. **Discussion questions** — 4–6.
9. **Further reading** — 3–5 items.

Recently I have started helping at my somewhat local church. The capacity in which I am serving is unlike anything I’ve done before, but there are perhaps many cross elements that pique my interests considerably. Most notably, I’ve been operating cameras two and three. I began operating camera three which requires less movement than  operating camera two. I did not anticipate the how much more challenging it would be to capture the correct shots on camera two than it was on camera three. Each church service, the shots that I am expected to capture mostly include those of the worship band, while camera three requires shots of worshiping parishioners from behind. I’ve thought about this and whether or not an attribution could be made with Professor Van Camp’s recent work on ballet reenactments [[[Longer Comments]]]. 

This activity got me to start thinking again about worship, and incorporating worship into even the most mundane aspects of our lives. For instance, lets consider what aspects of operating a camera may be considered an achievement of sorts. First, there are many possibilities for what we might think of as achievement.

However, for many, this typically seems to preclude certain activities associated with evangelical expressions of religious practice. Rather, that there are many aspects of evangelical religious expressions that represent a contradiction in terms, or that evangelical religious expression is in itself a contradiction in terms. More notably, this would include such activities as operating a camera during a religious service, or incorporating the prolific uses of cameras for use during what are supposed to be intimate (i.e., private) expressions of worship. For instance, one common and prevalent, and not altogether unjustified criticism, is that such activities are more associated with commercialism or perhaps capitalism, and less with authentic expressions of spiritual orientation, or observances of faith.

It’s a common caricature that philosophers notoriously make the simple complicated. 

> . . . because the point of philosophy is to start with something so simple as not to seem worth stating, and to end with something so paradoxical that no one will believe it. [[[THE PHILOSOPHY OF LOGICAL ATOMISM with Discussion]]]

I think that many philosophers have spent their entire careers trying to refute this common characterization. In a famous Platonic dialogue recounting Socrates’ trial in an Athenian court of law, Socrates articulates this common criticism:

> These earlier ones, however, are more so, gentlemen; they got hold of most of you from childhood, persuaded you and accused me quite falsely, saying that there is a man called Socrates, a wise man, a student of all things in the sky and below the earth, who makes the worse argument the stronger. [[[The dialogues of Plato]], 18c]

In part, I am tempted to argue that this is a matter of obligation for philosophers. Namely that before considering what our position is on some particular point, we should first attempt to defend every alternative position. As such, when it comes to American evangelicalism, we should first attempt to defend all positions for and against it. Assuming that this is true, then I should first evaluate by way of defense, arguments in favor of and against operating cameras two at three.

It should be noted that I do not necessarily think of philosophy as some privileged occupation because primarily, I think that in a free society, each member has an obligation to think critically. As such, each member in such a society ought to be a philosopher. It is true that perhaps you cannot pay everyone to think critically, I do not think that critical reflection can be equated in anyway with extrinsic goals, namely that it is just an intrinsic good to critically reflect. For instance, we often treat academic professions as a kind of achievement. But there are many within academia who perceive it as a service and sacrifice more so than  an achievement.

> > Professor Van Camp asks whether the ways in which contemporary audiences respond to historic ballet reconstructions and reenactments are important for our concepts of authenticity. It would seem that no, they are not. Three prominent examples of 19th century reconstructions do not appear to have appealed to audiences as their 19th century reenactment counterparts.
> > 
> > But this leads to an additional question, whether audiences think of these 19th century reconstructions as authentic and historically accurate. Perhaps it is not necessary here to make any comment on what is considered an audience. Some audiences desire to ‘view the past accomplishments of dance’ and others are more interested in imagining what a historical performance would look like when adding the benefit of ‘improved diet, health, and training regimes’. It is possible that the distinction here is one between compliance authenticity and ‘interpretive’ authenticity. It is not clear whether Professor Van Camp follows Andrew Kania in equating ‘authenticity’ with ‘genuine’ so that
> > 
> > First it is not clear what the necessary and sufficient conditions for something being authentic are. Professor Van Camp may accept the a ballet reconstruction as authentic simply if it compliance with the musical score. However, with ballet, which can contain both musical and choreographic scores, it would seem that there are additional factors one may consider when attempting to determine authenticity. However, this is only one kind of authenticity, namely that which complies with a musical score.
> > 
> > One possible reason why this is the case is because of the improved physical capabilities of today’s modern dancers.
> > 
> > ==“Traditionally, in dance, choreography is handed down from dancer to dancer in the studio, but we have to wonder if the “telephone game” plays a part in altering those transmissions.”== (Van Camp, p. 2)
> > 
> > Traditionally authenticity is determined by a musical score, but it is an open question regarding whether additional factors are also associated with authenticity. Do we only accept musical scores because we lack other reliable methods to determine the authenticity of a score?

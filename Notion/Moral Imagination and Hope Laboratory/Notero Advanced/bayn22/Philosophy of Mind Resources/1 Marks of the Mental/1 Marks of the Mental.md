## 1.2 The privacy of the mental

What does it mean to describe “the mental”?
    Mark of the Mental = property, collection of properties, sufficient for mentality:
		1. Privacy
			1. Everything that we would call “the mental” would have this property or collection of properties. Consider someone who is not yourself. Now lets imagine that they tell you that they have a headache. If “I have a headache” falls under the category of the “mental”, then there would be no way for you to know whether they in fact do have a headache. This is because you would have to either believe what they say, or interpret what they do as evidence for the fact that they have a headache. I lack the direct access to your headache that you have. Therefore your headache is private.
			2. But does this mean that privacy qualifies as a “mark of the mental?”
				1. Human beings may have access to their mental states, but does this mean that all minded creatures do? Probably not. [Forde & Wallesch 2003; Goldenberg et al. 1995]
				2. We do not always have direct access to many kinds of mental phenomena such as anger, depression or grief which might be more apparent to others than ourselves.
				3. Some mental states lack first-person access, e.g., our susceptibility to certain kinds of perceptual illusions, for instance representations that we ascribe to states for the same reasons someone else may describe them to us. 
			3. Another reason that privacy would not qualify as a mark of the mental is that it does not illuminate the nature of mentality.

## 1.3 Intentionality

Are all mental phenomena intentional?
    A state is intentional in virtue of the fact that it is directed towards a certain object, event, or state of affairs.
		- Desires: my desire for chocolate is directed at the chocolate.
		- Beliefs: my belief that Valentina Tereshkova was the first woman in space is directed at Valentina Tereshkova.
		- Emotions: my fear that the dog has rabies is directed at the dog currently foaming at the mouth.
    What are intentional states?
		- In other words, what is presented as perception, thought, and emotion?
			- objects: the object of my desire for chocolate is chocolate
			- propositional contents: propositions (i.e., a statement or claim that is either true or false) “foxes are solitary hunters”.
				- the object of my belief that Valentina Tereshkova was the first woman in space is Valentina Tereshkova, 
				- and the propositional content of my belief is ‘Valentina Tereshkova was the first woman in space’ is ‘Valentina Tereshkova was the first woman in space’.
			- objects of intentional states are publicly accessible
				- belief of Valentina = a particular Russian
				- desire to visit Madagascar = a particular island just east of Mozambique
			- objects of intentional states that are not publicly accessible
				- fictive imaginings: e.g., Santa Clause
				- hallucinations: for instance not sleeping
				- fear of monsters hiding under the bed
			- So what happens when the objects of intentional states are not ordinary physical objects? Say when they are monsters, irrational fears, or Santa Clause? 
				- I think that we can add virtual objects to this list as well, but more will need to be said about that later.
    How should we evaluate intentional states?
        For instance, beliefs vs. desires
			- beliefs: my understanding of the way the world is (indicative), beliefs have truth conditions
			- desire: my understanding of the way the world should be (imperative), desires have satisfaction conditions

# Unit 1 — Experience and doing

What is the fundamental nature of the mind?

Ancient - Medieval: Plato, Aristotle, Sankara, Ramnuja, Zhuangzi, Al-Farabi, Avicenna, Averroes, St. Thomas Aquinas, Willian of Ockham.

Early Modern: Rene Descartes, John Locke, and David Hume.

## The position (in my words)

But we’ll be more concerned with contemporary discussions of the nature of the mind and we will attempt to understand these questions in contrast to a more intractable philosophical problem, the problem of evil. 

For the purposes of this course, there are two primary approaches to the philosophy of mind:

Sciences of the Mind:
	- psychology
	- psychiatry
	- neuroscience

Philosophical:
	- Metaphysics: what exists, what kinds of categories we should include in our conception of reality
	- Epistemology: what we can know and how we can know it.
		- Can we know that other minds exist?
		- Is this an empirical question?
	- Philosophy of language: How do psychological terms acquire their meaning
		- feeling and emotion for instance
	- Philosophy of science: What is the relationship between the explanations provided by fundamental physics and those provided by the special sciences, biology - psychology
		- What do quarks or strings have to do with mental causation?
	- ethics and value theory: can we make sense of moral responsibility if there is no self?
		- Should we be responsive to another’s suffering if we can never fully experience whether another person has mental states?

## The standing objection

The boarder is contested. For instance, can empirical science ever solve some of the questions proposed by the philosophers, for instance, the problem of evil? If not, what does this say about those questions?

In her book, which we will reference quite a bit, [[Notion/Moral Imagination and Hope Laboratory/Notero Advanced/Wandering in Darkness/Wandering in Darkness]], Eleonore Stump gives an example of the ways in which this boundary is contested. 

Professor Stump is defending a response to the philosophical problem of evil which is this:

1. If God exists, then God is omniscient, omnipotent, and perfectly good. *(definition)*
2. An omniscient, omnipotent, perfectly good being would prevent suffering unless it had a morally sufficient reason to permit it.
3. There is no morally sufficient reason for such a being to permit the suffering we observe.
4. So if God exists, there would be no suffering. *(from 1, 2, 3)*
5. There is suffering — e.g. Bambi, who loses his mother young and must navigate the world without her.
6. Therefore God does not exist. *(from 4, 5)*

However, Stump revises this standard argument

| Reject | Who does this | What it costs |
| --- | --- | --- |
|   1. there is suffering<br>  2. that there is no mind | The philosophy of mind thread — no mind, no sufferer | You have to deny something everyone takes themselves to know first-hand |
|   1. There is such a God<br>  2. There is a mind | The atheist | The traditional conception goes |
|   1.  No morally sufficient reason<br>  2. There is a morally sufficient reason<br>  3. Or, that suffering for the sufferer can be redeemed | Stump | You have to say what the reason might be, or why we can’t expect to know it.<br><br>We can only see 3 through narrative |

So what does the problem of the mind have to do with the problem of evil? 
    If there is no mind, can we truly say that there is suffering?
    Professor Stump argues that the best response to this problem, is not analytic philosophy, but narrative. One approach that is important here is whether the best response to this problem is empirical rather than philosophical. Professor Stump distinguishes between analytic and what we can call narrative philosophy. I distinguish between empirical analysis and philosophical questioning.
    Consider Professor Stump’s response to the problem of suffering
    Claim: 
        Narrative makes a contribution to the philosophical reflection that
			1. suffering can be redeemed for the sufferer [who is] in personal relationship [with another], 
				1. So although suffering cannot be justified
				2. it can be redeemed
			2. [in other words] heartbreak can be woven into joy through the reciprocity of love. 
			3. So personal relationship [reciprocity of love], can redeem suffering
			4. Suffering can be redeemed for the sufferer through a personal relationship with God
        This reflection cannot be gotten as well, or at all, without the narrative.

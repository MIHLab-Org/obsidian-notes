## Introduction

Awe and wonder are distinct but psychologically related emotions. 

- Awe is an epistemic emotion that we experience when we perceive or conceptualize vastness and need to cognitively accommodate.
- Wonder is an epistemic emotion that we experience wonder when we glimpse at the unknown that lies just beyond our current understanding.
- Both emotions are important for the mental states related to knowledge. These include curiosity, doubt, surprise etc.
- Salient features of awe and wonder, chapter 3. 

## Outline

### 1. Freedom to Wonder

Awe is an epistemic emotion that we experience when we perceive or conceptualize vastness and need to cognitively accommodate. Wonder is an epistemic emotion that we experience wonder when we glimpse at the unknown that lies just beyond our current understanding. Both emotions are important for the mental states related to knowledge. These include curiosity, doubt, surprise etc. Salient features of awe and wonder, are part of our cultural environment, they have an evolutionary basis but are culturally scaffolded.

1. Chapters two and three look at philosophical and psychological perspectives on awe and wonder.
2. Chapter 2 traces the development of the view that philosophy is born in wonder and focuses special attention to views on the passions in early modern philosophy.
3. Chapter 3 places awe and wonder in an evolutionary context. It examines how awe and wonder enrich our lives. It focuses on three “human cognitive technologies, magic, religion, and science.
4. Chapter 4 looks at magic.
5. Chapter 5 considers religion as a cognitive technology that helps us to feel and rediscover awe and wonder.
6. Chapter 6 looks at science and the role of awe in scientific practice. Follows Jean-Paul Satre’s views on the emotions, awe plays an important role in scientific creativity.
7. Chapter 7 takes a look at Rachel Carson’s views on wonder and its role in our moral dealings with the environment.
8. Chapter 8 offers a reflection on why we should reclaim our sense of wonder.

### 2. That Sudden Surprise of the Soul: How Wonder Fuels Philosophy

Wonder is a motivating passion that urges us to do philosophy
    E.g., Nicolas Malebranche discovered Rene Descrates’ *Traite de l’homme* in a Paris bookstall. Disillusioned with the Aristotelian scholasticism that comprised a large part of his education, Malebranche describes emotions synonymous with exhilaration and joy. De Cruz argues that what is being described here is wonder which is central to what she calls a sense of firstness where we see something as wondrous or strange.
    Although rejected by Malebranche, a common feature in both Platonic and Aristotelian philosophy is a connection with wonder.
    Classical: Most, “Philosophy begins in wonder.”, Plato Theaetetus and Soophist c10, d1, d5, 19, Most
    Descartes: Wonder as Sudden surprise of the Soul

### 3. Mysteries of the Mind: The Psychology of Awe and Wonder

Emotions help biological organisms to respond effectively to the world they inhabit.

Humans only have a small repertoire of basic emotions that have evolved in response to adaptive problems.

But we have complex emotions that are not direct, evolved responses to adaptive problems, what are the functions of these?

These are grounded in culture and are grounded in culture and go beyond the basic emotions.

Awe and wonder are not basic emotions but help us to deal with situations that challenge what we think we know.

Humans have played games for at least 5000 years given the archeological evidence. The first video game is likely *Tennis for Two* created in 1958 by the physicist William Higinbotham at the Brookhaven National Laboratory. The first space game was a collaboration between [Steve Russell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steve_Russell_(computer_scientist)), Martin Graetz, Wayne Wiitanen, [Bob Saunders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Alan_Saunders), Steve Piner, and others and involved two ships being controlled by human players navigating the gravity well of a star.

Games have come a long way since then. The persistent universe / space simulations Eve Online, Star Citizen and Elite Dangerous are large scale open universe games. Eve Online for instance is notable for its very robust and complete player economy. The majority of the items that a player needs to successfully experience game play such as ships, weapons, ammunition, are provided by players who mine and manufacture raw materials to produce these items. 

Notably, these raw materials are only found (because they only spawn) in certain areas of space. These are areas that large player organizations fight one another to control. It is often the case that during conflicts such as these that a player’s character dies (i.e. a player looses the engagement). But what happens to the player afterwards? Well, these games use the “character regeneration” mechanic (respawning), which allow the player to continuing playing the game after a particular defeat without too much loss. 

Some have argued that this reduces one’s sense of wonder during gameplay and that removing this mechanic, also known as “permadeath”, will make gameplay more meaningful. # Promising References

> In this book, I will treat awe and wonder as distinct, but psychologically related emotions.

**Awe **is the emotion we sense when we perceive or conceptualize vastness, combined with a need for cognitive accommodation. Cognitive accommodation means we want to make space in our minds for this vast thing. Vastness can be physical (sheer size) or conceptual (e.g., complexity). Exemplars of things we can be in awe of include the night sky, a monumental, high building such as a temple or pyramid, a great and encompassing theory, superlative feats by a living being, and an astonishing mathematical result.

**Wonder **is the emotion that arises from a glimpse at the unknown terrain which lies just beyond the fringes of our current understanding. Like awe, it prompts a need for cognitive accommodation, but it does not necessarily have the dimension of vastness. Examples of elicitors include the intricacy of an insect seen under a microscope, an unusual fossil or strangely shaped crystal, and an unexpected astronomical event.

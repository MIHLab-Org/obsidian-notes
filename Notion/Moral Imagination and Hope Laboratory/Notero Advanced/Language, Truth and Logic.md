> Our charge against the metaphysician is not that be attempts to employ the understanding in a field where it cannot profitably venture, but that he produces sentences which fail to conform to the conditions under which alone a sentence can be literally significant Nor are we ourselves obliged to talk nonsense in order to show that all sentences of a certain type are necessarily devoid of literal significance.

Therefore:

- We need only formulate the criterion which enables us to test whether a sentence expresses a genuine proposition (literally significant) about a matter of fact, and then point out that the sentences under consideration fail to satisfy it.
- We shall first of all formulate the criterion in somewhat vague terms, and then give the explanations which are necessary to render it precise.

Criterion of Verifiability, Meaning

> We say that a sentence is factually significant to any given person, if, and only if, he knows how to verify the proposition which it purports to express—

that is, if he knows what observations would lead him, under certain conditions, to accept the proposition as being true, or reject it as being false.

- In order for a sentence to be meaningful
	- It must be a proposition, a statement that is either true or false
	- Utterer then asks “under what conditions is the statement true or false?”

---

Two senses of verification

> There are extraterrestrial lifeforms

- Practical verifiability
	- I can prove the claim
- Verifiability in Principle
	- I know what it would take to prove the claim

---

Strong and Weak Verification

- **Strong**: A proposition is said to be verifiable, in the strong sense of the term, if. and only if, its truth could be conclusively established in experience.
- **Weak**: But it is verifiable. in the weak sense, if it is possible for experience to render it probable.

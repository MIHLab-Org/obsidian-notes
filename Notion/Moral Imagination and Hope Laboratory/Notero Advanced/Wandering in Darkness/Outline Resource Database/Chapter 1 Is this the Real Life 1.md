Helen De Cruz argues that central to the human experience is a “continued thirst for the wondrous and the awe-inspiring”. What that means is that “awe and wonder are emotions that we harness by means of cultural practices”. She argues that we use cognitive technologies to do this. Hence art, religion, and philosophy.

We use David Chalmers' *Reality+: Virtual Worlds and the Problems of Philosophy* as our primary text, supplemented with classical philosophical works and contemporary readings. In this book, Chalmers uses one question to introduce the reader to the philosophical discipline, showing how many different philosophers working in many different areas, have attempted to solve this one question. We’ll be doing the same, but not by only focusing on the simulation question, but by focusing on this “cognitive technology” question. We’ll ask ourselves “what cultural practices actually meet ‘human’ needs and what are they?”

One way to understand Chalmers’ approach to philosophy in our primary text, is to say that he is asking whether or not the simulation hypothesis, the view that we could be living inside a simulation, is a viable one. 

### Simulation hypothesis?

How do we know we’re not in a simulation. In other words, how do we know that the world around us, as we experience it, is real?

But to only settle on this question, would be short sighted.

> Two years ago, the philosopher and veteran of the blogosphere [Eric Schwitzgebel](https://open.substack.com/users/105745695-eric-schwitzgebel?utm_source=mentions) published a lovely book titled [*The Weirdness of the World*](https://press.princeton.edu/books/hardcover/9780691215679/the-weirdness-of-the-world) — a book aimed at the public to show the mind-bending and strange views that have made it into current debates in philosophy, celebrating “a philosophy that opens” the horizon of our minds.

Some may interpret this approach in the wrong way. For instance, it might be thought that Walter Veit does so in his substack which I quoted above. He imagines that there are two kinds of philosophers, swallows and moles. Quoting Edourd Machery in a review of Schwitzgebel’s book:

> There are two kinds of philosophers: swallows and moles. Swallows love to soar and to entertain philosophical hypotheses at best loosely connected with empirical knowledge.

And he himself says as much. What does the word “philosophy” mean? It means “the love of wisdom”. The wisdom here is the process of not taking any assumptions for granted. There is no question that is too dumb, even the question about being in a simulation. In theory. In practice, we sometimes get overwhelmed when our current attention is undermined. 

For instance, a group of students are trying to understand what’s inside the oil used to fry McDonald’s fries. There are a number of suggestions floating around, but then someone comes and asks “What is frying oil?” 

This might *sound* like a dumb question. After all, we all know what frying oil is, what we want to know is what is inside McDonald’s frying oil. But the student’s question is not a dumb one. We have to first understand what exactly frying oil is before we can understand what is inside *McDonald’s *frying oil. But perhaps this is not a very good example. 

A better example is one in which we are all asking one another what is, objectively, the best sandwich. Well? What do *you* think is the best sandwich? In this scenario, we are the young Plato, and Socrates tells us that “objectively, the best sandwich is a slice of toast with salted butter spread across the top.” Is he right? I think that I would ask whether that would even constitute a sandwich?! Plato of course replies, “why not?” And now we’re doing philosophy.

This is what the philosopher does. A bunch of us are standing around thinking that we know enough to ask a certain question. The philosopher enters and points out that this is too fast.

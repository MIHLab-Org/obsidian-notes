Beliefs come in degrees between zero and one while a person has credences towards a belief.

Degrees of belief are better (have higher epistemic utility) when they are closer to the truth.

One should believe the truth, yet one at best has evidence for a given position, credence. It would be better for them to have credences, evidence that support true beliefs.
    If *p* is true, then the credence in *p* with the highest epistemic utility is 1, and if *p *is false, then the credence in *p* with highest epistemic utility is 0.
    so accuracy = closeness to the truth, therefore 
        ***truth rule:*** assign 1 to every truth and zero to every falsehood
    however, this describes luck, if we assign a credence function which *happens* to conform to the truth rule
        so the truth rule is not a very good plan
    so it is not about what degrees of belief are best to have, but rather what ***degrees*** of belief are best to plan to have
> My daughter is at the age where she’s dropping her nap. She takes an afternoon nap roughly half the time. On the days when she has a good nap, she is usually in a good mood in the evening. On the days when she doesn’t have a good nap, she is often really cranky. What I am wondering is what degree of belief I should ahve that she will be cranky this evening, *after* I find out whether [or not] she naps.

        truth rule: plan to be *sure* that she will be cranky if she really will be cranky, or else be *sure* she won’t be cranky if she really won’t be.
            if daughter naps, then in Nap-and-not-Cranky state so place all credence in that state
            if no nap, then no-Nap-and-not-Cranky state and place all credence in this state
            so either in nap or no-nap state
            truth rule would be inaccurate

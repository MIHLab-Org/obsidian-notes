> > “Questioning an animal 's status as "real" or "imaginary" was not part of the thought process of medieval bestiary readers.3 The bestiary was not a zoological work and was never intended to be used as such, even if much of the lore from the bestiary was eventually incorporated into earl y attempts at natural science. Instead, the bestiary was designed to impart a largely symbolic worldview based on Christological truths.4 Some animals were seen as reflections of Christ in the natural world, such as the lion, who would breathe on its dead cubs after three days to bring them to life (see plate 1), just as Christ died on the Cross and was resurrected three days later. Others replicated virtues or vices common to the human condit ion, such as the dragon, famed for killing elephants by using its tail to suffocate them (see plate 13), just as the faithful can become suffocated by sin. These animal stories were so widely disseminated in the Middle Ages that the entire conceptual framework surrounding animals and their assumed behaviors was influenced by the bestiary.” (Morrison and Grollemond, 2019, p. 4)

Abstract: "The law typically criminalizes not only completed crimes, but also attempts to commit them. This raises a number of questions that impinge on philosophy of action as well as on philosophy of law and moral philosophy. We must ask what should count as a criminal “attempt” and why we should criminalize such conduct. This raises broader questions about other kinds of “inchoate” crime: about how they should be distinguished and about why they should be crimes. We must ask why the law should attach the significance it does to whether or not the criminal result that an agent intends or expects to ensue actually ensues (one version of the problem of “moral luck”); why intention should be as crucial for criminal liability as it is in the law of attempts; and how “intention” should be defined. To answer such questions, we must engage at a deeper level with wider aspects of criminal law, for instance its proper aims. Should criminal law be primarily concerned with the prevention of harm, for instance, or with wrongful actions? If its proper concern is with actions, we then need to engage with philosophy of action and develop a conception of human action (and of the role of intention in action) that is appropriate in the context of criminal law."

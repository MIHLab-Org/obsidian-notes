Abstract: Only the most naive or tendentious among us would deny the extent and intensity of suffering in the world. Can one hold, consistently with the common view of suffering in the world, that there is an omniscient, omnipotent, perfectly good God? This book argues that one can.Wandering in Darkness first presents the moral psychology and value theory within which one typical traditional theodicy, namely, that of Thomas Aquinas, is embedded. It explicates Aquinas's account of the good for human beings, including the nature of love and union among persons. Eleonore Stump also makes use of developments in neurobiology and developmental psychology to illuminate the nature of such union. Stump then turns to an examination of narratives. In a methodological section focused on epistemological issues, the book uses recent research involving autism spectrum disorder to argue that some philosophical problems are best considered in the context of narratives. Using the methodology argued for, the book gives detailed, innovative exegeses of the stories of Job, Samson, Abraham and Isaac, and Mary of Bethany.In the context of these stories and against the backdrop of Aquinas's other views, Stump presents Aquinas's own theodicy, and shows that Aquinas's theodicy gives a powerful explanation for God's allowing suffering. She concludes by arguing that this explanation constitutes a consistent and cogent defense for the problem of suffering.

Consider the story of Mary Magdalene. 

We do not truly understand the centrality of her story to the nature of redemption until we understand the significance of her relationship with Jesus Christ. 

It isn’t so much that her relationship is significant of itself, but rather, by understanding what was taken away from her and then given back, do we understand how much she suffered in life. 

Mary Magdalene, when she met Jesus, was on her knees preparing to be die. 

Her death was not due because of some unique indiscretion that she only is guilty of. 

Her death would have been political only. 

We are told that she was caught in adultery, but we are never told with whom. 

The Christ stumbles unto this scene and noticing the further political benefit this fact represented, the leaders demanding her execution attempt to squeeze even more political influence through it. 

They are going to catch the Christ in a moral conflict. 

The law says that she should be executed for adultery, the leaders know that the Christ would offer redemption. 

They thought this would be impossible thereby resulting in the conflict. 

The Christ does offer redemption, just not in the way they supposed. 

We come back to the present tense and the Christ’s body is being lowered from the cross where Mary supposes that He was just taken from her. 

We do not know the depth of what she suffered until Mary, whose story seems fairly insignificant until this point, is the first person whom the Christ chooses to reveal himself to in his resurrected body.

But in this story, we can imagine a place where Jesus was not crucified. 

In this version of the story, Mary doesn’t suffer. 

Rather, Christ redeems her from execution, and then she follows Him from town to town as He redeems others. 

What place then, does the suffering have in Mary’s redemptive arch? 

Eleonore Stump, in her book *Wandering in Darkness*, wants to explain the purpose of Mary’s suffering. 

Mary’s redemptive arch, she wants to argue, necessarily includes the suffering.

## Otherworldly Redemption

Suffering: shame and wrongdoing leave a person alienated from themselves. 

The alienation is opaque where the victim doesn’t even recognize their suffering.

1. Knowledge by acquaintance. Narrative and the knowledge of persons: mirror neurons and second-personal, i.e., Franciscan, knowledge

2. Second-Personal Knowledge

		2. Paula’s personal interaction with Jerome is direct and immediate
		3. Jerome is conscious
	2. Mirror neuron is central to knowing the emotions of another person.

Abstract: "ABSTRACT How should you decide what to do? Orthodox decision theory offers attractively simple advice: Do whatever has the most expected value. In what follows, I argue that this is not always good advice for agents like us. Agents like us are susceptible to error: Sometimes, we do not succeed at doing what is recommended by the advice we are trying to follow. For agents susceptible to error, there can be situations in which trying to do what they expect to produce the most value will be expected to leave them worse off. I will discuss why situations like this arise and give some concrete examples. I will also identify a condition on agents' credences, which is both necessary and sufficient for maximizing expected value to be the best advice for them to try to follow. However, crucially, this condition will not always be satisfied by agents like us."

### 3.5. Plan-Based Decision Problems

What advice should an agent follow?
    Depends on how well an agent can expect things to go if they try to follow a plan with [certain properties]

A plan-based decision problem, $\Delta = \langle\hat{\Delta}, [\cdot], P\rangle$ is a tuple consisting of the following:
	- An opaque decision problem, $\hat{\Delta}$.
	- A bijective function, $[\cdot]$, on $S^{\hat{\Delta}}$.
	- A state-independent probability function, $P$, defined on $\hat{S}^{\hat{\Delta}}_{[\cdot]}$

With a given plan-based decision problem and plan, $\pi$, that is assigned an expected value $EV(\pi)$, we can evaluate advice since $EV(\pi)$ as the value an agent expects to produce by trying to follow $\pi$ as evaluated from their prior perspective. This is calculated as follows:

$$
EV(\pi) = \sum_{s \in S}P(s)\cdot\sum_{s' \in S}P([s']|s) \cdot \upsilon(\pi(s')(s)).
$$

In order to calculate the expected value of a plan, for any pair of states, we need to find three quantities where the expected value of a plan is the sum of the products of these three quantities, for each ordered pair of states. A plan $\pi$ is EV-optimal in a decision problem $\Delta$ iff for all $\pi’$ $\in$ $\Pi$: $EV(\pi) \geq EV(\pi’)$.
	1. Your prior credence of being in that state
	2. Your prior credence of acting in the second as though you were in the first
	3. The value of the act your plan recommends in the second, when performed in the first

$MEVI$: plans ***to*** maximize expected value, recommends, for each state, an act which has optimal act expected value relative to the agent’s posterior credences at that state.

$MEVII$: Plans ***which ***maximize expected value, recommends, for each state, an act which has optimal *act expected value* relative to the agent’s posterior credences at that state. A plan which maximizes expected value has optimal plan expected value relative to the agent’s prior credences.

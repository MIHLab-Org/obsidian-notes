# Section 1: Research Design and Methodology

## 1.1 Overview and Conceptual Framework

MIHL proposes a novel interdisciplinary program that uses open-world space simulation gaming as a structured environment for cultivating moral reasoning and argumentation. Drawing on experimental moral psychology, virtue ethics, and educational theory, MIHL situates gameplay not as entertainment but as a moral laboratory — a space where we encounter genuine ethical dilemmas, exercise practical wisdom, and develop the habits of reflective moral agency.

The project is anchored in the Templeton Foundation’s priority of Moral Imagination and Hope: the conviction that character development requires not only exposure to moral content but the practiced ability to imagine, argue for, and enact a better world. Open-world games like Star Citizen, Elite Dangerous, and EVE Online are uniquely suited to this purpose. Unlike scripted narrative games, these titles generate emergent ethical situations — faction allegiances that demand loyalty or betrayal, economic decisions with community-wide consequences, and moments requiring honesty, courage, or restraint under social pressure — that no designer fully predetermined and no participant can anticipate.

## 1.2 Game Environments as Moral Laboratories

Two primary game titles anchor the laboratory experience:

- **Star Citizen** presents participants with a persistent universe governed by a reputation economy, a player-driven legal and crime system, and multi-crew cooperative missions. Participants must navigate questions of trust, complicity, distributive justice, and collective action — often under time pressure and social influence from peers. The game’s “crime stat” system makes moral choices consequential: lawbreaking carries compounding social and material costs, modeling real-world virtue dynamics.
- **Elite Dangerous: Odyssey** offers a galaxy-scale simulation of trade, exploration, faction politics, and conflict. Participants encounter questions about resource distribution, environmental stewardship of celestial bodies, and the ethics of war between rival powers. Its on-foot gameplay expansion adds interpersonal dynamics — deception, alliance, betrayal — at human scale within a cosmic context.
- **EVE Online** presents perhaps the most morally complex player-driven universe in gaming. Its player-run corporations and alliances function as genuine social institutions — complete with internal governance, diplomacy, betrayal, and warfare on a civilizational scale. The game is notable in academic literature for the spontaneous emergence of economic and political norms among players, making it uniquely valuable for studying how moral frameworks develop under conditions of freedom, scarcity, and competing interests. Participants in MIHL will engage EVE Online with particular attention to questions of collective responsibility, institutional ethics, and the tension between self-interest and the common good — themes central to Templeton’s interest in free societies and character development.

All three titles are chosen because they reward long-term thinking, cooperation, and reputation — virtues Templeton has identified as central to free societies and flourishing communities. None of the games resolves moral dilemmas for the player; participants must reason through them and live with the consequences across subsequent sessions.

## 1.3 Research Questions

The graduate research assistant (GRA), embedded in an interdisciplinary program in experimental moral psychology, will investigate the following primary questions:

- Does structured gameplay in open-world simulations measurably improve adolescent capacity for moral reasoning and argumentation, as assessed by validated instruments (e.g., Defining Issues Test, Moral Foundations Questionnaire)?
- Which in-game moral dilemma types (distributive, relational, civic) produce the greatest transfer to real-world ethical reasoning tasks?
- Do debrief-and-argumentation sessions following gameplay episodes strengthen moral reasoning more than gameplay alone?
- How do variables such as age, prior philosophical exposure, and peer group composition moderate outcomes?

## 1.4 Program Structure and Session Design

During the pilot year, MIHL will operate one cohort of approximately 12–15 high school participants. Each cohort completes a structured 10-week program:

- **Weeks 1–2 (Orientation):** Participants are introduced to the game environments, basic controls, and the philosophical frameworks guiding the program. Undergraduate RAs facilitate an introductory seminar on moral reasoning, practical ethics, and the concept of moral imagination.
- **Weeks 3–8 (Core Gameplay Sessions):** Twice-weekly 90-minute sessions in the lab. Each session begins with a structured scenario prompt — a moral dilemma framed in the game’s universe — and concludes with a 20-minute facilitated debrief in which participants are required to articulate and defend the ethical reasoning behind their in-game choices. Undergraduate RAs observe, log behavioral data, and facilitate argumentation using Socratic questioning techniques.
- **Weeks 9–10 (Assessment and Reflection):** Post-program moral reasoning assessments are administered. Participants complete reflective written exercises connecting their in-game decisions to real-world ethical concepts. A final group session invites participants to present their most significant moral decision point from the program and argue for its ethical justification before peers.

## 1.5 Data Collection and Research Instruments

Undergraduate RAs serve as trained research implementers under the supervision of the GRA. Data collection includes:

- Pre/post administration of validated moral reasoning scales
- Session observation logs capturing decision points, reasoning patterns, and peer influence dynamics
- Audio/video recording of debrief sessions (with IRB-approved consent) for qualitative discourse analysis
- In-game behavioral telemetry extracted from Star Citizen, Elite Dangerous, and EVE Online APIs, tracking decision frequency, cooperation/defection rates, and reputation trajectories
- Semi-structured exit interviews with participants

All data collection instruments and protocols will be reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the partner university prior to participant recruitment. Given that participants are minors (ages 14–18), enhanced consent procedures — including separate minor assent forms and parental/guardian consent — will be implemented.

## 1.6 University Partnership and Lab Infrastructure

MIHL will operate out of a dedicated Gaming and Philosophy Laboratory housed within an existing esports facility at a partner academic institution.

- **Primary target partner:** Saint Louis University (SLU)
- **Status:** Partnership development is substantively underway. Dr. Reynolds completed a test installation of Star Citizen within SLU’s esports lab infrastructure, demonstrating technical feasibility and reflecting active goodwill of SLU personnel.
- **Redundancy:** Additional esports-equipped institutions in the Saint Louis metropolitan area are under consideration as secondary options.

The university partnership serves three functions: (1) academic credibility and IRB infrastructure, (2) integration of the GRA into a recognized interdisciplinary doctoral program, and (3) a pathway for expansion into undergraduate coursework and graduate seminars at SLU over time.

The lab will house five high-performance gaming stations, each configured with monitors, peripherals, and headsets. The physical space will support both immersive individual gameplay and collaborative group debrief, with flexible seating that can reconfigure between the two modes within a single session.

# Section 2: Evaluation and Dissemination Plan

## 2.1 Evaluation Framework

MIHL will employ a mixed-methods evaluation design combining quantitative pre/post outcome measurement with qualitative process documentation. Evaluation serves two audiences: (1) the internal research team, which uses findings to iterate on program design, and (2) the broader scholarly and practitioner community, which benefits from publishable results about gaming as a vehicle for moral development.

The evaluation is structured around three levels:

- **Participant-Level Outcomes:** Changes in moral reasoning capacity, argumentative quality, and self-reported moral identity will be measured using validated instruments before and after each 10-week cohort cycle. Effect sizes will be calculated and compared across cohort iterations.
- **Program-Level Fidelity:** Undergraduate RAs will complete session fidelity checklists documenting the degree to which each session adhered to the designed protocol. Deviations will be logged and analyzed to identify which program elements are most reliably implementable across different participant groups.
- **Research-Level Rigor:** The GRA will conduct a formal analysis of all collected data at the conclusion of each academic year, producing an annual research report submitted to the PI and, where appropriate, prepared for peer-reviewed publication.

## 2.2 External Evaluation

In year two of the project (should renewal funding be secured), MIHL will engage an external evaluator — an independent researcher in moral psychology or educational assessment — to conduct a third-party review of program outcomes, methodology, and data integrity.

## 2.3 Dissemination Plan

MIHL is committed to making its findings accessible to three distinct audiences:

- **Academic community:** The GRA will submit at least one peer-reviewed manuscript per year of the grant to journals such as Journal of Moral Education, Ethics, or Simulation & Gaming. The PI will present findings at relevant conferences (e.g., Association for Moral Education annual meeting and relevant philosophy of education symposia).
- **Practitioner community:** MIHL will produce one practitioner-facing white paper per year summarizing findings in accessible language for educators, school counselors, and youth program designers. These will be distributed through partner school districts and made freely available on the program website.
- **Public audience:** The program will maintain an active public web presence documenting its work, including video documentation of (consented) lab sessions, participant reflections, and a blog authored jointly by the PI and GRA connecting program activities to broader questions in moral philosophy and public life.

## 2.4 Sustainability and Scaling

MIHL is designed with post-grant sustainability in mind. The university partnership creates a natural institutional home for the lab beyond the initial funding period. Revenue pathways under active development include tuition-bearing professional development workshops for educators, a fee-based summer intensive for high school students, and licensing of the curriculum and data collection framework to partner institutions.

By the conclusion of the 12-month grant period, MIHL aims to have:

- Completed one full pilot cohort (12–15 participants)
- Established a formal university MOU
- Produced a preliminary research report and at least one conference presentation
- Generated proof-of-concept data sufficient to support a renewal or larger-scale grant application (including a future full proposal to the John Templeton Foundation)

# Section 3: Team Qualifications

## 3.1 Principal Investigator and Lab Director

Dr. Montaque Reynolds, Ph.D., serves as Principal Investigator and founding Lab Director of the Moral Imagination and Hope Laboratory. Dr. Reynolds holds a doctorate in Philosophy from Saint Louis University (2024), a Master’s in Philosophy from Oklahoma State University, and a Bachelor’s in Philosophy from Seattle Pacific University. Dr. Reynolds currently serves as Visiting Assistant Professor of Philosophy at Stetson University in DeLand, Florida, teaching logic, aesthetics, and philosophy of law. Areas of specialization include Social and Moral Epistemology and Philosophy of Mind, with competencies in Ethics, Meta-ethics, and Aesthetics.

Dr. Reynolds’ scholarship focuses on the moral expression of sentiment in fiction, social identity, and the relationship between aesthetic experience and moral agency. Dr. Reynolds’ peer-reviewed work appears in Experiments in Moral and Political Philosophy (Taylor & Francis, 2024), with additional manuscripts under review at Vernon Press.

Critically for this project, Dr. Reynolds is already engaged in directly relevant applied work. As Director of Education at the BGG Foundation (MMO Gaming Ethics), Dr. Reynolds oversees programming at the intersection of multiplayer online gaming and ethical development. Dr. Reynolds’ active Saint Louis University SCHF project investigates how interactive digital platforms — specifically massively multiplayer online games — can influence critical thinking, ethical reasoning, and moral decision-making among underserved youth. Additional technical capacities include natural language processing, R, Python, and AI model evaluation.

## 3.2 Co-Investigator — Aesthetics and Philosophy of Art

Stephen Snyder, PhD, serves as Co-Investigator with a focused portfolio in aesthetics and the philosophy of art. Dr. Snyder is a visiting scholar whose research spans philosophy of art, social and political philosophy, and nineteenth and twentieth century continental philosophy.

Within MIHL, Dr. Snyder’s contribution is focused on: (1) co-authoring peer-reviewed publications arising from the research program, and (2) co-developing the aesthetics component of the philosophical curriculum.

## 3.3 Graduate Research Assistant — Experimental Moral Psychology

The graduate research assistant (GRA) will be recruited from an interdisciplinary doctoral program with specialization in experimental moral psychology, ideally with secondary training in philosophy, education, or cognitive science. The GRA serves as the primary research methodologist for the project, responsible for the design and administration of assessment instruments, quantitative and qualitative data analysis, and preparation of scholarly outputs (conference presentations and peer-reviewed manuscripts).

## 3.4 Undergraduate Research Assistants — Education Implementation

Two undergraduate research assistants, recruited from the Education program at Saint Louis University or a peer institution in the Saint Louis metropolitan area, will serve as the primary facilitators of the MIHL session experience. They implement the philosophical curriculum, facilitate post-session debrief and moral argumentation exercises, build sustained relationships with participants across the 10-week cohort cycle, and collect observational and survey data under the supervision of the GRA.

## 3.5 Office and Program Administrator

A part-time Program Administrator will manage the operational infrastructure of the laboratory, including participant scheduling and recruitment logistics, IRB compliance documentation, consent and assent processes, funder reporting, and general office administration.

## 3.6 University Partner and Institutional Affiliation

Saint Louis University serves as the primary target institutional partner for MIHL. The agreement will cover space access, personnel arrangements, indirect cost rates, and intellectual property. If required at the time of submission, Dr. Reynolds can provide a letter of intent from the SLU Department of Philosophy or the Office of Sponsored Programs.

## 3.7 Interdisciplinary Strength of the Team

MIHL is structured to align with Templeton’s Study of Virtues strand, drawing on insights from multiple fields including the social sciences, humanities, technology, and education. Together, the team spans philosophy, psychology, education, and digital simulation in a configuration that is genuinely interdisciplinary rather than merely multidisciplinary.

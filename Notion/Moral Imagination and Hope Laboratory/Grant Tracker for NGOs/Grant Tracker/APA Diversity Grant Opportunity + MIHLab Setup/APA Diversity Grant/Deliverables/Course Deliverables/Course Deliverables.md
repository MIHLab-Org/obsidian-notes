## Description

The Moral Imagination and Hope Laboratory (MIHLab) uses open-world video games (Star Citizen, Elite Dangerous) to make philosophical reasoning embodied, testable, and personal. Instead of asking “What is a healthy relationship?” in abstraction, students *act as* someone trying to navigate a morally complex scenario. They develop intuitions through roleplay, test those intuitions through game mechanics, and reflect on what they’ve learned.

Philosophy as a discipline has a well-documented demographic problem: students from privileged backgrounds with early exposure to abstract reasoning are far more likely to pursue philosophy and these more important questions, while first-generation, lower-SES, and under-represented students are systematically filtered out before they ever encounter the discipline.

At Stetson University, this pattern is acute. Our student population is predominantly first-generation, working-class, and from under-resourced K-12 systems. Traditional philosophy instruction—lecture-based, emphasizing abstract principles and formal logic—privileges one learning modality: the verbally/linguistically fluent, already-socialized-to-academic-discourse learner.

Moreover, when philosophy *is* taught abstractly (“What is justice? Define it.”), it feels remote from students’ lived experience. It’s something done *to* them, not something they *do*.

**The Consequence:**

Philosophy remains demographically homogeneous. Students at institutions like Stetson never see themselves as potential philosophers. The discipline loses voices, perspectives, and reasoning traditions it desperately needs.

**Our Intervention:**

The Moral Imagination and Hope Laboratory (MIHLab) uses open-world video games (Star Citizen, Elite Dangerous) to make philosophical reasoning embodied, testable, and personal. Instead of asking “What is justice?” in abstraction, students *act as* someone trying to navigate a morally complex scenario. They develop intuitions through roleplay, test those intuitions through game mechanics, and reflect on what they’ve learned.

This approach:
- Lowers barriers for students outside the philosophy pipeline
- Reaches diverse learning modalities (kinesthetic, spatial, narrative)
- Makes philosophical thinking feel like something *they can do*
- Creates a replicable model for other under-resourced institutions

This is fundamentally a diversity intervention because it expands *who gets to do philosophy*. By making philosophy embodied and testable, we invite students who might otherwise never participate.

## Assignment 1 - Epistemology

## 🔭 Observation

*What intuition, counter-argument, or disagreement prompted this experiment? Be specific — what do people persistently claim or feel that you want to test?*

Where does the mind stop and the rest of the world begin? Most people say that the mind ends at the boundary of the body. “What is outside the body is outside the mind”. This experiment will allow us to test Dave Chalmers’ ***active externalism*** according to which the mind is in part made up of features of a person’s environment.

Consider the following example by David Chalmers in his book ***Reality+.*** 

> 
> Otto has Alzheimer’s and uses a notebook to write down key facts and recalls them later by looking them up in his notebook. Inga remembers facts with her brain in the normal way. One day, Otto wants to go to the Museum of Modern Art, so he looks up the address in his notebook and sets out for 53rd street. Inga also wants to go to the Museum of Modern Art and so recalls the address from her brain and sets out too. Chalmers argues that Otto’s notebook is a part of his brain in the same way that Inga’s biological memory is. The notebook acts as a repository of Otto’s beliefs about the world, he believes that the museum is on 53rd street because it is written in his notebook—just as Inga believes this because it is stored in her brain.

## ⚙️ Method — Star Citizen

1. *Structure the experiment in three phases. Each phase is a sub-page.*
	- **Phase 1: The Internalist Baseline (Manual Labor)**
		- **Scenario:** Sans epistemic aids
		- In this phase, the student must perform a task without "epistemic aid."
	- **Phase 2: The Coupled Pilot**
		- **Scenario:** The Precision Instrument Landing
		- Instead of a giant, unmistakable crater, the student is tasked with finding a station at a set of Planetary Coordinates (Latitude/Longitude).
		- **Objective**: Demonstrate how external symbols (HUD elements) drive cognitive processes in real-time, creating a "Coupled System" where the ship and pilot function as a single unit.
	- **Phase 3: The "Broken Mind" (The Contra-Positive)**
		- This scenario presents a moment where the student experiences the loss of behavioral competence when the external component is removed.
		- **Goal**: Return to space station turning off the HUD once the station is in view.
		- **The Task:**
			- Start the navigation normally using the ships automatic navigation system (The Extended Mind in action).
			- Once the student can see the station, the student must disable their HUD display (CTRL+ALT+G) while continuing the flight.
2. **The Philosophical Result**: Without the HUD, the pilot still knows how to fly and navigate (Internalism), but they can no longer "think" effectively about the target's distance, lead, or health, or their own speed and distance to the station’s entrance. Their "behavioral competence" has dropped because their Extended Mind was severed.

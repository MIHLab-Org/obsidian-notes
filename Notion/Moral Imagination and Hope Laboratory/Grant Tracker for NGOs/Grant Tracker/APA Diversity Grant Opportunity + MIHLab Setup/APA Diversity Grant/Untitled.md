| Abstract (please note that there is a word limit of 300 words.)* | Philosophy as a discipline has a well-documented demographic problem: students from privileged backgrounds with early exposure to abstract reasoning are far more likely to pursue philosophy, while first-generation, lower-SES, and under-represented students are systematically filtered out before they ever encounter the discipline.<br>At Stetson University, this pattern is acute. Our student population is predominantly first-generation, working-class, and from under-resourced K-12 systems. Traditional philosophy instruction—lecture-based, emphasizing abstract principles and formal logic—privileges one learning modality: the verbally/linguistically fluent, already-socialized-to-academic-discourse learner. Students who think spatially, kinesthetically, or through narrative find philosophy inaccessible.<br>Moreover, when philosophy *is* taught abstractly ("What is justice? Define it."), it feels remote from students' lived experience. It's something done *to* them, not something they *do*.<br><br>Philosophy remains demographically homogeneous. Students at institutions like Stetson never see themselves as potential philosophers. The discipline loses voices, perspectives, and reasoning traditions it desperately needs.<br><br>The Moral Imagination and Hope Laboratory (MIHLab) uses open-world video games (Star Citizen, Elite Dangerous) to make philosophical reasoning embodied, testable, and personal. Instead of asking "What is justice?" in abstraction, students *act as* someone trying to navigate a morally complex scenario. They develop intuitions through roleplay, test those intuitions through game mechanics, and reflect on what they've learned.<br>This approach:<br>• Lowers barriers for students outside the philosophy pipeline<br>• Reaches diverse learning modalities (kinesthetic, spatial, narrative)<br>• Makes philosophical thinking feel like something *they can do*<br>• Creates a replicable model for other under-resourced institutions |

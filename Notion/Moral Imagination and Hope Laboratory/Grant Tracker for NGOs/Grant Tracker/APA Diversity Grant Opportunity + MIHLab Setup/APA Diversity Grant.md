## About the grant

Diversity and Inclusiveness Funding

The American Philosophical Association, in keeping with its mission and goals and the association’s longstanding commitment to addressing philosophy’s serious lack of demographic diversity, will make available up to $20,000 in fiscal year 2027 to fund one or two projects aiming to increase the presence and participation of women, racial and ethnic minorities, LGBTQ+ people, people with disabilities, people of low socioeconomic status, and other underrepresented groups at all levels of philosophy. The APA board of officers strongly prefers proposals that convincingly demonstrate their potential to decisively impact diversity and inclusion within philosophy.

[Learn more about diversity and inclusiveness grants and how to apply.](https://www.apaonline.org/page/diversity_fund)

[See past projects funded by APA grants.](https://www.apaonline.org/page/fundedprojects)

For questions, contact [grants@apaonline.org](mailto:grants@apaonline.org).

# Small Grant Fund

Each year, the APA's small grant fund, financed by the Eastern Division, makes up to $25,000 in grants of normally no more than $5,000 each. The board of officers considers applications for these grants each year at its fall meeting in November.

[Learn more about the small grant fund and how to apply.](https://www.apaonline.org/page/grantfund)

[See past projects funded by APA grants.](https://www.apaonline.org/page/fundedprojects)

For questions, contact [grants@apaonline.org](mailto:grants@apaonline.org).

[All APA members are invited to submit proposals.](https://www.apaonline.org/page/grants) **The deadline to submit a proposal for either the diversity and inclusiveness fund or the small grant fund is June 30, 2026.**

The board of officers will review proposals and make funding decisions in November 2026. Proposed projects should commence no earlier than January 1, 2027. The APA will disburse grant funds by June 30, 2027.

Please note: It is APA policy that a given project can only be submitted to one APA funding stream at a time.

## Funding priorities

As the APA operates primarily in the US and Canada, programs must have a clear application to diversity in a US/Canada context. The board wishes to express its particular interest in supporting programs that explicitly address more than one type of diversity (e.g., gender and race/ethnicity). The board will give preference to projects based at under-resourced institutions.

## Funding amount

$20,000 in fiscal year 2027 to fund one or two projects aiming to increase the presence and participation of women, racial and ethnic minorities, LGBTQ+ people, people with disabilities, people of low socioeconomic status, and other underrepresented groups at all levels of philosophy.

## 2. PROBLEM STATEMENT

### Draft

**Title:** "Democratizing Philosophical Reasoning: Embodied Pedagogy for Students Outside the Philosophy Pipeline"

**The Problem:**

Philosophy as a discipline has a well-documented demographic problem: students from privileged backgrounds with early exposure to abstract reasoning are far more likely to pursue philosophy, while first-generation, lower-SES, and under-represented students are systematically filtered out before they ever encounter the discipline.

At Stetson University, this pattern is acute. Our student population is predominantly first-generation, working-class, and from under-resourced K-12 systems. Traditional philosophy instruction—lecture-based, emphasizing abstract principles and formal logic—privileges one learning modality: the verbally/linguistically fluent, already-socialized-to-academic-discourse learner. Students who think spatially, kinesthetically, or through narrative find philosophy inaccessible.

Moreover, when philosophy *is* taught abstractly ("What is justice? Define it."), it feels remote from students' lived experience. It's something done *to* them, not something they *do*.

**The Consequence:**

Philosophy remains demographically homogeneous. Students at institutions like Stetson never see themselves as potential philosophers. The discipline loses voices, perspectives, and reasoning traditions it desperately needs.

**Our Intervention:**

The Moral Imagination and Hope Laboratory (MIHLab) uses open-world video games (Star Citizen, Elite Dangerous) to make philosophical reasoning embodied, testable, and personal. Instead of asking "What is justice?" in abstraction, students *act as* someone trying to navigate a morally complex scenario. They develop intuitions through roleplay, test those intuitions through game mechanics, and reflect on what they've learned.

This approach:

- Lowers barriers for students outside the philosophy pipeline
- Reaches diverse learning modalities (kinesthetic, spatial, narrative)
- Makes philosophical thinking feel like something *they can do*
- Creates a replicable model for other under-resourced institutions

**Why It Matters for Diversity:**

This is fundamentally a diversity intervention because it expands *who gets to do philosophy*. By removing the linguistic/abstract requirement and making philosophy embodied and testable, we invite students who might otherwise never participate.

Author argues that imaginative resistance occurs when epistemic norms of consistency come into conflict with an author's prescriptions.

But a key qualifier of this claim is that the divergence occurs when we realize that we've mistaken some concept employed by the author of a fiction.

So while this does seem to be a problem with epistemic and not non-epistemic norms as the author points out, it is hard to see how the norm that was violated was a truth norm.

> I-belief is used to keep track of information about events, characters and their motivations, and other fictional details that the author supplies us with (Friend, 2017).

However imaginative resistance is a series of cases where i-Beliefs do not constrain our imaginative capacities. 

But we might add, they do not contextually constrain our imaginative capacities.

Two explanations:
- Impossibility Hypothesis
	- inability to imagine impossibilities
- Unwillingness Hypothesis
	- We are unwilling to allow ourselves to i-believe morally dubious claims

Both theories involve non-epistemic constraints
- Do not concern the truth value of the claims in question

But we might ask "what is a fictional truth value?"

Consider epistemic norms:
- Norms
- Background beliefs

1. Consistency
	1. beliefs must be consistent with each other
2. Truth norms
	1. what I believe is true
	2. must test claims against what I know

			1. changes our i-beliefs about the fiction
		2. genre-sensitive
			1. whether the object is fiction or myth changes my acceptance
		3. differs from person to person

### Three Basic Forms of Skepticism

#### Pyrrhonian Skepticism:
	Universal skepticism.
> The Pyrrhonian skeptics sought suspension of judgment as a way of achieving calm (ataraxia) in the face of seemingly intractable disagreement. 
#### Humean Skepticism:
> Thus, our belief that the sun will come out tomorrow may be based on our beliefs that it came out yesterday, and the day before, and the day before that, and so on. Those premise beliefs, as we might call them, do not deductively entail that the sun will come out tomorrow. We certainly have available now more sophisticated inferential bases for believing that the sun will come out tomorrow than a mere inductive enumeration. We know that the solar system is relatively stable and will remain in its present state of equilibrium for billions of years. But that scientific knowledge is itself the result of inductive inferences. [https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/skepticism/]
		
#### Cartesian Skepticism:
> The subject’s belief that there are hands in front of her is in that case true, but she still doesn’t know it. 

## Epistemological Problems of Skepticism

> Two main types of response to the skeptical argument have traditionally been given: a metaphysical response that focuses on the nature of the world, perceptual experience, and/or the relation between them, in an effort to show that perceptual knowledge is indeed possible; and a more directly epistemological response that focuses on principles specifying what is required for knowledge and/or justification, in an effort to show that skepticism misstates the requirements for knowledge.

Metaphysical response to skepticism:
* Nature of the world
* Perceptual Experience
* Relation between Nature of the World and Perceptual Experience
Epistemological Response to Skepticism:
* Principles specifying what is required for
	* Knowledge
	* Justification
* Skepticism misstates the requirements for knowledge
	* If we can specify what knowledge requires, then we can defeat skeptical arguments.

#### Indirectness Principle

First, the problem of the external world begins with the observation that extra-mental objects are never directly present to the mind.

Cartesian Realism:
	Objects external to the mind cause what occurs in the mind.

Two Reponses:
	Thomas Reid's	Realism:
		> [The theory of ideas] is directly contrary to the universal sense of men who have not been instructed in philosophy. When we see the sun or moon, we have no doubt that the very objects which we immediately see, are very far distant from us, and from one another. . . . How are we astonished when the Philosopher informs us, that we are mistaken in all this; that the sun and moon which we see, are not, as we imagine, many miles distant from us, and from each other, but that they are in our own mind; that they had no existence before we saw them, and will have none when we cease to perceive and to think them; because the objects we perceive are only ideas in our own minds.

There were a group of philosophers around the fifth century B.C.E who we generally refer to now as the 'Atomists'. These individuals defended a view we'll call 'physicalism' which held that all reality is material. For many years between then and now, thinkers inspired by Christian and other religious values generally rejected this view and defended something called dualism according to which, not all reality is material since human beings at least possess immaterial souls *and* physical bodies. Times have changed and although it was espoused well over two millennia ago, thinkers inspired by the scientific method of empiricism, semantic meaning can only be conferred through empirical observation, largely once again defend physicalism. Richard Swineburne is one crazy modern day academic who bucks this trend and actually defends dualism! 

You might be asking what the importance of this is and why am I talking about it while displaying images of game play taken from a video game. Consider the game Star Citizen, whose images you are now seeing. SC was devised by the game developer Chris Roberts and developed by Cloud Imperium Games. Certainly like any other game studio, CIG would like for Star Citizen to be self-sustaining, meaning to generate enough income to cover its expenses. In order for it to be such, it would need to bring in enough income to a) justify its development to investors like most other entertainment franchises, or b) continually bring in an income that allows it to develop software updates as the underlying hardware develops. 

In CIG's case, the public buys ships as a means of support for the continual development of the game. Thus far, this has been a fantastic system. It allows CIG to cater the development of SC directly to the audience that will derive the most enjoyment from, read intrinsically benefit of, the game's continual development. 

However, because we are talking about audiences and 'stake holders', it might be important to talk about the personal identities of those stake holders. You see, there is an interesting mechanic in SC that tries to explain *why* a player can continue playing after death. We call this the death mechanic and what it is is an attempt by the developer to explain why a player can continue playing after their character experiences a death. You see, without this mechanic, the player would have a couple of options, select and develop a new character, which many would balk at, or stop playing the game altogether. It has generally been difficult for a developer to get rid of the death mechanic altogether. There are examples of attempts to do so include versions of Minecraft and No Man's Sky but these are rarely popular with gamers. 

What the death mechanic in SC does is explain that the player's character's consciousness has been uploaded to a computer server, and when the player experiences death, the character's body is regenerated and their consciousness is downloaded into this regenerated body. What this mechanic does explain is why the player can keep playing as that character. What it doesn't explain is how that character is the same character.

This brings us back to the introduction regarding physicalism. You see, in SC, when a player dies, it would not make sense to perfectly model real life. As perfectly good little phsyicalists, all reality is material. So you see, in real life when a person dies, they cease to exist. If we are to treat real life like a game, then there are several ways to understand this death. On the one hand, we might consider it a failure. For instance, the person who dies is that person who failed without completing the game. But we rarely argue this. We often treat end of life ceremonies as a kind of celebration. But of course this depends on the kind of life that person lived. If the life was a short one, then we might think their death tragic, that they passed away too soon. I am not sure, and am not ready to consider whether we might think that their life too, was a tragic one.

But what is the difference between the lives of other who pass away too soon and those whose deaths are celebrated. So actually, this raises several paradoxes all which are only loosely related to the question of personal identity. Succinctly, we call these loosely related problems: characterization, personhood, persistence, evidence, population, personal ontology, and survival. It would be nice if these problems were clearly related either to that of personal identity or to one another, but unfortunately for us, they are not. Rather they represent a wide range of problems that are only loosely connected either to each other, or to personal identity, and not always very well differentiated in the literature.

Lets return briefly to the discussion on dualism vs. physicalism. You see, when we apply questions about whether a death was tragic or a cause for celebration, what we are really asking is how to define the thing at the center of the discussion. Philosophers call this broad problem the problem of Personal Identity. These are philosophical questions about ourselves that arise in virtue of our being people, or better yet: persons. For you see, on the one hand what we want to know is "what does it mean to begin to exist". On the other what we want to know is "what does it mean to cease to exist"?

So this problem has many sides. On one side, generally, if a person passes away, we would like to think that properties or traits of character that are used to explain who that person is, or was, would cease to be a causal explanation for why some event or another occurred. This might be called the problem of characterization. We can imagine a set of goals and possible future actions that were never instantiated because of the death of the individual. If we can explain the existence of CIG to Chris Roberts' tenacity, superior focus, and dogged determinism, then various projects whose existence was caused by these traits, should too cease to exist. For instance, if Chris Roberts had passed away before ever realizing his intended goals for SC.

If Chris Roberts were to unfortunately pass away tomorrow, there are various projects related to CIG and SC that should stop and sometimes this does happen. For instance, we might think that a tragic death is one that does not inspire some future project with which the person either themselves identified with, or that others identified with them. Analogously, of if we were to use SC to model the real world as close as possible. 

Imagine that CIG were to develop a death mechanic as close as possible to reality. Each time a player's actions resulted in the death of their character, various goals that the player pursued through the character would also need to be given up. This is sometimes easy for a developer to implement. For instance, lets say that a player commits themselves, and thereby the character they are playing, to say transporting cargo from one planet to the next. If they are attacked by pirates and killed, the character would reasonably be unable to continue to pursue the transport of that specific cargo to the destination. This is an easy way for the developer to model the real world through their game and does not run into any strictly obvious problems.

However, sometimes this is not how the real world is. For instance in the case of Martin Luther King, when he was assassinated, various projects associated with his name, only then were completed. This would be because some aspect or character trait possessed by King was, but shouldn't have given his eventual non-existence, was a causal factor in the continued completion of these projects, e.g., the passage of civil rights legislation in the United States.

But this might not work for another question which philosophers ask about personal identity is what it *means* to be a person? In the example above, what it means to be a person is to have traits that have as some kind of causal influence on past, present and future events. Here, we might think that while the trait which belonged to Martin Luther King was a casual factor in the beginning of the discussion about civil rights, it was the traits as belonging to other individuals who knew him, that explains their eventual passing. This objection makes it a point to deny that Martin Luther King is responsible for the passage of civil rights legislation, only the discussion surrounding civil rights legislation. 

The problem of personhood then as related to questions of personal identity asks what "it means to be a person vs non-person". Here, it is not enough to possess characteristics that explain why something happened. For instance, we might say that to be a person is to be tenacious. King's tenacity explains the passage of civil rights legislation and therefore, even though King was assassinated, his tenacity explains the passage of civil rights legislation and therefore King is still a person.

But this approach also raises another problem, the problem of persistence. What does it mean for a person to persist from one moment to the next. If we are only relying on King's tenacity to explain his personhood, then this raises the problem that before King was tenacious, King didn't exist. Further, King's tenacity persisted even after his assassination. We know this since we can cite his tenacity as a causal facture in events that occurred after his assassination. Ergo, King was still a person even after his assassination.

But notice that this raises other problems as well. Consider the death of our character. Well obviously we might think that we ourselves do not possess a personal identity inside a video game. But lo and behold! There are character traits that we possess that explain why something or another occurred in Star Citizen. For instance, while we did not complete our cargo transport, we did get as far as the moon on Yela and so our personhood remains intact even inside Star Citizen. Our personhood persists across the material-digital divide! As such, might be well justified in thinking that neither our physical deaths, nor the transition from the physical to the digital will bring our existence to an end!

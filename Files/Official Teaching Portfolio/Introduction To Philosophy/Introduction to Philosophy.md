# Course Description

In this course we use David Chalmers' book *Reality+: Virtual Worlds and
the Problems of Philosophy* to look at questions humans have been asking
for thousands of years. While the primary focus of the book is one
question in particular — "Are we in a simulation?" — we use that question
to frame others:

- What does it mean to know that the sky is blue?
- Is murder really wrong?
- How did I come to know that murder is wrong?
- What is real?
- Can a virtual world be a real world?

We will not only look at contemporary issues about AI and simulation
theory, but will also examine how philosophers of the past posed and
attempted to answer these questions. In doing so we will explore
epistemology (theory of knowledge), metaphysics (the nature of reality),
philosophy of mind, and ethics.

A primary objective of this course is to teach students how to construct
good arguments and how to assess the arguments of others.

This summer section uses **Elite Dangerous** as a philosophical laboratory.
Three structured experiments — completed outside class during assigned
windows — test Chalmers' arguments directly: you will navigate permadeath,
disable your HUD on atmospheric entry, and manipulate mission stakes to
examine whether virtual experience, cognition, and emotion are genuine.

## Assignment Rubric

All written assignments (experiments, Reflective Analysis, Essay) are
graded on three structural dimensions plus internal criteria.

### Structure (applies to all written work)

| Layer | What the instructor looks for |
| --- | --- |
| **Introduction** | A brief context sentence (what is this about?); one sentence stating what another philosopher says; one sentence stating what the student thinks; a brief structured "why" that references a philosopher the student agrees with. |
| **Body** | The interlocutor's argument in full (claim → premise 1 → premise 2 → conclusion). Then the student's developed response: claim, premise 1 with two supporting statements, restatement of premise 1, premise 2, etc. Experiments function as the support — e.g., "Chalmers says cognition is extended. I tried to fly in Elite Dangerous without the HUD, proving I use the HUD as an extension of my cognitive activity. Therefore Chalmers is right." |
| **Conclusion** | Revisits and summarizes what the student did. |

### Internal Criteria

**Thesis**

- **Excellent (full):** A single, clear statement of the main point.
- **Good (3/4):** Thesis is present and fairly obvious, but not in one sharp sentence.
- **Needs Improvement (1/2):** Thesis must be reconstructed from throughout the paper.
- **Unacceptable (1/4):** No real thesis.

**Premises**

- **Excellent:** Every reason clearly stated; sub-arguments provided for controversial claims; basic premises plausibly true.
- **Good:** Premises mostly clear; sub-arguments appear for controversial points.
- **Needs Improvement:** Premises hard to identify; missing sub-arguments.
- **Unacceptable:** No real premises, or premises very likely false.

**Support**

- **Excellent:** Premises clearly and strongly support the thesis; argument is logically valid or makes the conclusion very plausible.
- **Good:** Premises support the thesis reasonably well.
- **Needs Improvement:** Premises only weakly support the thesis; argument is invalid.
- **Unacceptable:** Premises do not support the thesis.

**Counter-Arguments**

- **Excellent:** Thoughtfully addresses both obvious and less-obvious objections with original replies.
- **Good:** Addresses the most obvious objections with reasonable responses.
- **Needs Improvement:** Misses obvious objections, or responses are weak.
- **Unacceptable:** Ignores objections and opposing positions entirely.

## Pilot Assessment Write-Up — Assigned Today (Wed June 18)

Due: **Mon July 7** (Canvas)

Write **800–1,200 words** describing and analyzing the Basic Flight
tutorial as a simulation in Chalmers' sense. Use the four section
headings below. Draw explicitly on *Reality+* Chapters 2–3 — cite at
least three specific ideas or page references.

**Section 1 — What is it? (Describe the simulation)**
What does the Basic Flight tutorial represent? (A starship cockpit, a
pilot, the physics of moving through space, a region of the game world.)
What is the simulation doing — to the ship's behavior, and to you as a
trainee? Tie this to Ch. 2: "What is a simulation?" and "What does it
do?" What do you now "know" as a result of the simulation?

**Section 2 — Is the simulation a good one? (Evaluate it)**
What makes this a good simulation? Consider fidelity (how faithfully it
mirrors what it models), interactivity, feedback, and immersion.
What is deliberately missing or simplified? If the goal is to train a
real pilot-skill using a ship that does not exist, does "good simulation"
mean "realistic," "useful," or both? Defend your answer.

**Section 3 — What level of simulation is it?**
Place the tutorial using Chalmers' discussion of the phases/levels of
simulation (and Baudrillard's four phases, Ch. 2). Is it a faithful copy
of something real, a simulation of something that never existed (a
fictional starship), or something in between? Connect to Ch. 2's
treatment of possible worlds and to Nozick's experience machine — how
is a flight trainer like, and unlike, the experience machine?

**Section 4 — Could you tell from the inside? (The Knowledge hook)**
When you close the game you know you were in a simulation. But suppose
you could not step out. Using Ch. 3, ask: could you prove from inside
the tutorial that it was a simulation? Connect to Descartes' dream and
evil-demon arguments, Putnam's brain-in-a-vat, and the Knowledge
Question.

**Key terms to use:** simulation · the four phases of simulation ·
fidelity · possible world · the experience machine · the Knowledge
Question · the Reality Question · dream argument · brain-in-a-vat ·
*cogito, ergo sum*

**Format:** 800–1,200 words, double-spaced, 12-point font, your name
and date at top. Use the four section headings. Attach your three
screenshots. Cite by page number (e.g., "Chalmers, p. 23").

## Experiment 1 — Death and Permadeath in Elite Dangerous

**Window: Complete anytime July 13-19. Due on Canvas**

**Philosophical Question**
Does the possibility of permanent, irreversible loss make virtual
experience more meaningful? If stakes were real — if death in the game
meant losing your progress permanently — would the experience feel
more genuine?

**Background**
Before running this experiment, complete the Week 1 and Week 2 readings
(Chalmers, Chs. 1–7). Both argue that virtual experience is genuine. We
will test that claim by introducing genuine stakes.

**Procedure**

*Part A — Standard death:* Accept a medium-difficulty mission (combat
or exploration). At some point during the mission, allow your ship to be
destroyed. Do not resist. Observe the respawn sequence. Note what you feel.

*Part B — Reflective permadeath:* Accept another mission of similar
difficulty. Before starting, commit to the following rule: *if I die, I
will delete my save and start over.* You do not have to actually delete
your save — but make the commitment genuine in your imagination. Attempt
the mission. Note what changes.

*Part C — The thought experiment:* Now consider: what if your grade in
this course depended on your commander surviving the semester? Would the
experience feel different? How?

**What to Notice**
Did your behavior change between Part A and Part B? Did your emotional
state change? Did the virtual world feel more or less real when genuine
loss was possible?

Use Chalmers' language and concepts throughout. Your write-up demonstrates
how well you understand the arguments.

## Experiment 2 — Extended Cognition and Atmospheric Entry

**Window: Complete anytime July 11–17. Bring your log to class July 14.**

**Philosophical Question**
Does the HUD (heads-up display) extend your mind into the ship — or
does it replace your mind? When you remove it, are you more or less
present in the virtual world?

**Background**
Andy Clark and David Chalmers argued in "The Extended Mind" (1998) that
cognitive processes can extend beyond the brain into tools and
environments. The HUD in Elite Dangerous is a cognitive tool — it
provides altitude, speed, heat, and structural integrity in real time.
Without it, you must navigate by sight, sensation, and judgment alone.

This experiment uses atmospheric entry (planetary landing approach) as
the test environment because it requires precise attention to altitude
and angle — exactly the kind of task the HUD is designed to support.

**Procedure**

*Part A — HUD enabled:* Enter glide toward a planet's surface with the
HUD fully active. Navigate the approach. Note what information you are
attending to and how you are making decisions.

*Part B — HUD off:* Enter glide again, this time disabling the HUD
(Options → Graphics → HUD). Navigate by visual cues alone. Note what
changes in your attention, your decision-making, your sense of presence.

*Part C — Reflection:* Which approach felt more like you were flying the
ship? Which felt more like the ship was flying itself?

**Safety Note**
Entering glide without the HUD is difficult. You may crash. That is fine.
Crashing is data.

**Write-Up Template**

Use Chalmers' language and concepts. Your write-up demonstrates your
understanding of the extended mind thesis (Ch. 16).

*Observation: What intuition are you testing? (e.g., Clark and Chalmers
say the mind can extend into tools — but does it feel that way?)*

*Hypothesis: "I claim that X." Example: "The HUD functions as an
extended cognitive system — removing it reveals which cognitive tasks
the player has genuinely internalized."*

*Materials: HUD options in Elite Dangerous; planetary landing approach;
glide mechanics.*

*Method:*

| Phase | Elite Dangerous |
| --- | --- |
| Baseline (Part A — HUD on) | Complete a Task with the HUD |
| Extended Case (Part B — HUD off) | Remove the HUD |
| Contra-Positive (Part C — Reflection) | Remove the HUD during the task. |

*Results: What did each phase reveal?*

*Conclusion: Did the experiment support the extended mind thesis? What
does this mean for your argument?*

## Weekly Reflection 5

## Experiment 3 — Risk, Respawn, and Moral Emotions

**Window: Complete anytime July 25–31. Bring your log to class July 28.**

**Philosophical Question**
Does the structure of risk in a game determine whether the emotions it
produces are genuine forms of knowledge? Do low-stakes games produce
shallow emotions while high-stakes games produce genuine ones?

**Background**
Nussbaum argues that emotions are appropriate when they track something
real and valuable. A trivial loss produces a trivial emotion. A genuine
loss produces genuine grief. We will test this by manipulating the
stakes of gameplay.

**Procedure — Four Conditions**

| Condition | Mission Risk | Respawn | What to Notice |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | High risk | On (standard) | Do you play more or less carefully? What do you feel when you survive or die? |
| B | Low risk | On (standard) | Does the ease change your emotional engagement? |
| C | High risk | Off (imagined permadeath) | How do genuine stakes change your experience? |
| D | Low risk | Off (imagined permadeath) | Can a low-stakes mission feel meaningful with permadeath consequences? |

You do not need to complete all four conditions if time does not permit —
complete at least two (A+C or B+C) and reflect on the contrast.

**Note on Conditions C and D**
You do not have to literally delete your save. Before starting the
mission, say to yourself: *If I die, I start over.* Mean it as much
as you can.

for personal academic purposes and may not be shared without the
professor's written consent. Violation will be reported to the Office
of Community Standards.

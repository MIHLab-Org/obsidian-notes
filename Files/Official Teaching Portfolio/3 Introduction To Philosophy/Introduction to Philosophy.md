# Course Description

A primary objective of this course is to teach students how to construct good arguments and how to assess the arguments of others.  In this course we use David Chalmers' book *Reality+: Virtual Worlds and the Problems of Philosophy* to look at questions humans have been asking for thousands of years. While the primary focus of the book is one question in particular — "Are we in a simulation?" — we use that question to frame others, it also uses Chalmers' approach to help us understand our own commitments. 

This summer section uses **Elite Dangerous** as a philosophical laboratory. It includes three structured experiments completed outside class during assigned windows. Through these experiments, we will test Chalmers' arguments directly: you will navigate permadeath, disable your HUD on atmospheric entry, and manipulate mission stakes to examine whether virtual experience, cognition, and emotion are genuine.

We will not only look at contemporary issues about AI and simulation theory, but will also examine how philosophers of the past posed and attempted to answer these questions. In doing so we will explore our own commitments including, epistemological (theory of knowledge), metaphysical (the nature of reality), philosophy of mind, and ethics.

# Elite Dangerous Experiments

This course uses three structured experiments in Elite Dangerous to test
Chalmers' philosophical claims from the inside. Each experiment is a
take-home assignment completed during an assigned window and written up
using the experiment template on Canvas.

**The key principle:** experiments and weekly reflections must use
Chalmers' language and concepts. The experiments demonstrate the extent
to which you understand the material and the arguments. A strong
experiment write-up shows: here is what Chalmers (or another philosopher)
claims; here is the experiment I ran to test it; here is what I found;
here is what that means for the argument.

1. Main claim from Chalmers or someone else. 
2. The student’s intuition regarding that claim. 
3. The experiment student ran to test that claim. 
4. The students finding. I.e., whether they’ve changed their position or not. Why?

**Experiment Windows**

For full instructions, see the experiment briefings in the Weekly
Schedule and on Canvas.
